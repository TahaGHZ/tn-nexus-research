## Extracts

Abstract (PDF p. 2):

> The main findings show that within Tunisian trade as a whole, informal trade accounts for only a small share (5 percent of total imports). However, informal trade represents an important part of the Tunisia's bilateral trade with Libya and Algeria, accounting for more than half the official trade with Libya and more than total official trade with Algeria. The main reasons behind this large-scale informal trade are differences in the levels of subsidies on either side of the border as well as the varying tax regimes.

> This issue will become even more significant if fuel prices in Tunisia rise again as a result of a reduction in the levels of domestic subsidies.

Introduction, printed p. 4 (PDF p. 6):

> However, it is possible to estimate that roughly 25% of the fuel consumed in Tunisia is in the form of informal imports from Algeria. The main reasons behind this large-scale informal trade are differences in the levels of subsidies on either side of the border as well as the varying tax regimes. For example, the price of fuel is around one-tenth in Algeria of that in Tunisia.

Footnote 8, same page:

> This is based on data from STIR showing fuel imports of 2,790 million tons as against national fuel consumption of 3,746 million tons (source: www.stir.com.tn). We used an estimated weight of 0.792 tons per cubic meter of fuel (source: www.unitjuggler.com).

Same page:

> Fuel is cheaper, but government revenues are reduced, not only because goods are not subject to customs duties at the Tunisian border, but also because traders avoid paying value-added tax (VAT) provided they remain within the informal network.

Printed p. 21 (PDF p. 23), "Extrapolation of Estimates of Informal Trade in Fuel from Algeria":

> If we hypothesize that 60% of the 3,000 vans involved in informal trade are carrying fuel and that each one makes only a single trip each day with an average load of 1.6 m3 over a 320-day year, we can estimate that the amount of fuel imported informally is 921,600m3 per year (or 3,000x0.6x1.6x320), with a sales value of around TND 882m (at $95 per barrel).

Footnote 31, same page:

> According to media in Algeria, 1.5 million of m3 of fuel would be sold in Morocco and Tunisia. Based on the population differential between Morocco and Tunisia, fuel smuggled to Tunisia would be at least 500,000 m3 (Econostrum 2013).

English gloss: the World Bank estimates that informal trade is about 5% of Tunisia's total imports
but more than total official trade with Algeria. Roughly 25% of fuel consumed in Tunisia came in
informally from Algeria, where fuel cost about one-tenth of the Tunisian price. A field-based
extrapolation for the Algerian border gives about 921,600 m3 of fuel a year (about TND 882 million).
A press-based lower bound is 500,000 m3.

## Notes

The 25% figure is derived in footnote 8 from the gap between STIR imports and national
consumption. "Million tons" is an evident typo for thousand tons (the figures match a market of
about 3.7 Mt). The footnote does not explain how a gap between imports and consumption measures
smuggling, since domestic refining (about 1.7 Mt/yr nameplate, SRC-TG-312) could fill much of it.
Treat the 25% as an order of magnitude, not a measurement. The van-count method rests on
assumptions stated by the authors (3,000 vans, 60% carrying fuel, one trip a day). ONEM later
describes the various attempts to quantify smuggled fuel, including this World Bank study, as
"très divergents" (SRC-TG-130). The data refer to about 2012-2013. Tier B to match SRC-TG-162
(a World Bank working paper, not peer reviewed).
