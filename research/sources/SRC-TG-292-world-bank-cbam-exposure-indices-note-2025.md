## Extracts

Note p. 1 (PDF p. 8), definition:

> A trade partner's exposure to the EU CBAM depends on two key factors: competitiveness loss, measured by carbon cost intensity relative to that of an average EU producer, and trade and economic dependence on the EU in affected sectors. Excess carbon payments are computed by valuing the differences in emission intensity between exporting countries and the EU for the relevant product exports to the EU at prevailing carbon prices.

Note p. 2 (PDF p. 9):

> 1. Trade Exposure Index (TEI) —excess carbon payments in proportion to the value of exports in a specific CBAM sector;

> This note presents key findings using World Bank WITS mirror trade data from 2022 and emission intensity data from Chepeliev et al. (2025) and Chepeliev and Corong (2022) benchmarked to 2017.

> Several countries display high values on the Trade Exposure Index (TEI) in CBAM-affected sectors: Mozambique in aluminum (7 percent), Belarus in cement (27 percent), Egypt in fertilizer (19 percent), and India in iron and steel (13 percent).

> Overall trade exposure to EU CBAM is expected to be limited. [...] Developing economies' economic exposure to CBAM is expected to be small. [...] Other countries have exposures below 0.1 percent.

Note p. 6 (PDF p. 13), carbon price assumption:

> In this paper, the notional value of CBAM certificates is set at USD100 per metric ton CO2, which is in the range of recent European Union Allowance (EUA) futures trading. The indices are proportional to the value of the price of EU ETS allowances; all indices would be twice (half) as large if the emissions price was twice (half) as large.

## Notes

Static exposure estimate, not a forecast: it values 2022 trade flows with 2017-benchmarked
country-average emission intensities at a notional USD 100/t CO2, assuming full phase-in. The
authors warn that firm-level emissions may differ from country averages and that domestic carbon
prices paid can be credited. Tunisian values are shown only graphically.
