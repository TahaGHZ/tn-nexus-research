## Extracts

Printed p. 2 (PDF p. 11), para. 5:

> Natural gas and oil respectively account for 53 and 47 percent of Tunisia's primary energy demand in 2018. In terms of electricity generation, 83 percent derives from open and combined cycle gas turbines, 15 percent from dual fuel steam units (natural gas and heavy fuel oil) and 2 percent from renewables, mostly wind.

> More than half of natural gas, 72 percent of which is used for electricity production, is imported from Alegria; part of this is paid as gas transit fee. As a result, overall dependency on energy imports reached 51 percent in 2018 (compared to 7 percent in 2010) and will likely continue to increase in the future, reducing Tunisia's energy security. The dependence on a single source of gas supply makes Tunisia extremely vulnerable to supply interruptions.

Printed pp. 2-3 (PDF pp. 11-12), para. 5, continued:

> Projections anticipate a shortage of primary energy, particularly natural gas, starting in 2023.

English gloss: in 2018, 83% of generation came from open- and combined-cycle gas turbines, 15%
from dual-fuel steam units (gas and heavy fuel oil) and 2% from renewables. More than half of
the gas, 72% of which goes to power, comes from Algeria, partly as transit fee. Energy import
dependency was 51% in 2018 against 7% in 2010. Relying on one gas supplier makes Tunisia very
vulnerable. Projections expected a primary energy shortage, especially of gas, from 2023. The
document cites the ministry's Conjoncture Energétique for December 2018 for these figures.

Printed p. 3 (PDF p. 12), para. 7:

> STEG is responsible for electricity service throughout the value chain, for transmission and distribution of natural gas, and, since 2015, for gas imports from Algeria. In 1996, the generation segment was opened to independent power producers (IPPs), which can produce and sell electricity to STEG. Currently, 18 percent of electricity supply is provided by one IPP; 82 percent is generated by STEG and a small amount through industrial self-generation.

English gloss: STEG has handled gas imports from Algeria since 2015. Generation was opened to
IPPs in 1996. At the time of writing, one IPP supplied 18% of electricity and STEG 82%.

Printed p. 4 (PDF p. 13), para. 9:

> Fuel purchases and IPP payment account for more than 80 percent of the cost of electricity and are highly dependent on international oil price fluctuations, to which the gas import from Algeria is indexed, as well as exchange rate variations.

English gloss: fuel and IPP payments make up more than 80% of electricity cost. They follow
international oil prices, to which Algerian gas import prices are indexed, and exchange rates.

Printed p. 5 (PDF p. 14), para. 11:

> GoT plans to develop two new gas-fired combined cycle power plants (Skhira 1 and 2) of approximately 500 MW each, the first as a public-sector plant by 2022, and the second as an IPP in subsequent years. These plants are essential to provide grid stability as significant RE is integrated in the power system.

English gloss: the government planned two new gas-fired combined-cycle plants at Skhira, about
500 MW each, the first public by 2022 and the second as an IPP later, presented as needed for
grid stability as renewables are added.

Printed p. 10 (PDF p. 19):

> CPC is the only IPP in Tunisia's power market, owning and operating a 471 MW gas-fired power generation facility in Radès, which accounts for 19 percent of power supply in the country.

English gloss: Carthage Power Company (CPC), the only IPP, runs a 471 MW gas-fired plant at
Radès that supplies 19% of power.
