## What it is

SONEDE is building reverse-osmosis seawater plants to supply drinking water to coastal and southern areas. Djerba (50,000 m3/day, extendable to 75,000) started in 2018 at 169 million dinars (SRC-TG-271). Zarat in Gabes (50,000 extendable to 100,000 m3/day) had works 2019-2024 at 371 million dinars and entered operation in 2024, per SONEDE (SRC-TG-272). Sfax (100,000 extendable to 250,000 m3/day, about 1 billion dinars, JICA) started units from 20 July 2024 and entered the operation phase on 4 October 2024 (SRC-TG-273). Sousse was "under construction" in 2023 and, in a press report of 6 July 2026, "bloquée en phase d'essais techniques approfondis" (SRC-TG-277, SRC-TG-464). Its capacity differs by source: 50,000 m3/day (SRC-TG-279, 2021), 100,000 (SRC-TG-277, 2023); unresolved. Together the first three are up to 200,000 m3/day, about 73 million m3/year at full use (our arithmetic; actual output is not published). Eau 2050 plans 192 million m3/year in 2030 and 265 million from 2035 (SRC-TG-276). A 2025 consultancy inventory cites an ambition of 775,000 m3/day by 2035, without a source (SRC-TG-278).

## What it claims, and what the evidence shows

- **More water, independent of rain.** Supported at plant level: the Djerba study found a net rise in supply and better quality (SRC-TG-660). Not independently checked: how much Sfax and Zarat actually deliver. The Djerba study's data period is 2015-2020 and its publication year is not printed on the journal page.
- **A last resort.** Eau 2050 records SONEDE's view that desalination is a "dernier recours" (SRC-TG-276). The same strategy raises volumes from 18 million m3 in 2020 to 265 million (SRC-TG-276, SRC-TG-503). The two statements sit side by side in the scenario.
- **Renewable coupling.** A stated goal (SRC-TG-276, SRC-TG-279). Not evidenced: GIZ (2021) says seawater desalination is mainly fossil-powered and names one 212 kW solar unit for a 50 m3/day station (SRC-TG-661). We found nothing on the power supply of Sfax or Zarat; that is a data gap, not proof of absence.
- **Energy recovery.** Sfax uses pressure exchangers (SRC-TG-273). No measured kWh/m3 for any Tunisian seawater plant was found. Figures in circulation: 3 kWh/m3 (SONEDE energy director, SRC-TG-279; GIZ estimate, SRC-TG-661), 3.5 (JICA planning assumption) and 4.2 (Djerba design ceiling) (SRC-TG-275), 2.4 (used in FND-TG-271). None is a measured operating value.

## Audit

**Root-cause coverage (1).** FND-TG-501 traces the gap to groundwater exploitation above 100% of resources, irrigation using about 80% of water, and tariffs disconnected from cost (SRC-TG-503). Desalination touches none of these. It only adds drinking-water supply on the coast. It does answer one cause named in FND-TG-451, the foreseen peak-day deficit of 1.6 m3/s from 2020 for Greater Tunis, Cap Bon, the Sahel and Sfax (SRC-TG-402), though late. Score 1: a real answer to a symptom, not to the root causes.

**Evidence quality (1).** Evidence is mostly the operator's own pages, a trade-press quote and one interview-based study. No independent performance, energy or brine data was found for Sfax or Zarat. The cost figure (about 3 dinars/m3 for seawater, energy 40% of cost, in a passage on the Sfax plant) is an unsourced estimate in a hydrogen report (SRC-TG-661).

**Adoption (2).** Three plants run (Djerba, Zarat and Sfax, per SONEDE pages), Sousse is stuck, and the 2035 ambitions are far above what exists. Delivery is slow: The May 2021 statement that Sousse would come online "next year" did not hold (SRC-TG-279, SRC-TG-464).

**Side effects (1).** High electricity use on a mainly gas-fired system (SRC-TG-661; shares in FND-TG-101, not re-checked here), brine, and financial strain on SONEDE. No measured marine impact was found, so the score reflects known mechanisms and one reported figure, not demonstrated harm.

**Cost and accessibility (1).** About 3 dinars/m3 (SRC-TG-661) against an average selling price of 0.94 dinars in 2021 (SRC-TG-482), with a uniform national tariff that KfW says implies cross-subsidy toward high-cost sites (SRC-TG-277). Capital comes from foreign loans (KfW, AFD, JICA) that the State repays. The 3 dinars is not a comparable like-for-like cost series.

## Limits and blockers

- **Not a demand solution.** The programme leaves irrigation, about 80% of resources and "far from operating at its optimum" (SRC-TG-503), unchanged. Water at about 3 dinars/m3 cannot economically go to irrigation.
- **Network.** SONEDE's own 2017 estimate: recoverable real losses of about 86 million m3, about 20% of consumed and billed volume and more than the capacity of the four seawater plants then under way (SRC-TG-402). KfW says much of the energy-intensive water is lost through leaks (SRC-TG-277).
- **Finance (economic).** SONEDE cost coverage was 71% in 2019 and 97% in 2022; KfW expects desalination to add to its burden (SRC-TG-277). The tariff deficit is FND-TG-476.
- **Delivery (institutional).** The ministry's 2017 report calls for accelerating the ongoing projects (SRC-TG-402); Sousse is still in testing (SRC-TG-464).
- **Legal and regulatory.** No law text was read for brine discharge or for renewable-supply obligations, so none is cited. See outstanding verification.
- **Energy supply.** Gas dependence is FND-TG-100/101/102 (not re-read here; SRC-TG-661 says supply is mainly natural gas, then oil). Each added plant raises load on that system. Any coincidence of summer electricity and water peaks (FND-TG-220) is unconfirmed in this record.

## Criticisms

- KfW 2023 (SRC-TG-277): financial burden and network losses. Supported by its SONEDE figures. It evaluates the brackish programme and extends the point to seawater plants.
- World Bank CCDR (SRC-TG-482): average energy cost of SONEDE drinking water rose from 0.82 TND/m3 (2015) to 1.30 (2021) after its brackish and seawater plants came on line, while the average selling price rose from 0.56 to 0.94; this is an energy-cost series, not total cost, and is not seawater-specific. Supported.
- Academic concern over brine (SRC-TG-660). Partly supported: reported by interviewees, not measured.
- Civil-society criticism (desalination displacing demand-side measures) was seen in a search summary only, not opened, and is not recorded here.

## Trade-offs and consequences

- **Energy.** SONEDE's director said the whole utility used about 500 GWh/year, with Djerba alone at 40 GWh and 200,000 m3/day more coming (SRC-TG-279). FND-TG-271 gives an illustrative 460 GWh/year at 2030 scenario volumes. Both are statements or illustrations, not measurements. Observation: electricity is almost all gas. Projection: energy at 35% of SONEDE costs by 2030 (the director's forecast).
- **Water and agriculture.** Reliable coastal drinking supply may reduce the need to cut irrigation for drinking (the 2023 ban on SONEDE water for agriculture is from FND-TG-451, not re-checked here), but we found no evidence on whether desalination relieves aquifers. That link is an argument, not an observation.
- **Marine environment.** The Gulf of Gabes seabed and Posidonia are already damaged by phosphogypsum (SRC-TG-662). Added brine is a mechanism of concern. No brine monitoring or impact study was found for Zarat or Sfax. The water lane owner should open a phase 1 finding on coastal brine discharge; no existing FND matches, so `creates` is empty.
- **Public finance.** Foreign-loan financing and recurring SONEDE losses add to the State's burden (FND-TG-476).

## Verdict and gap

Partially-solves: extra drinking water on the coast answers part of FND-TG-451 (coastal capacity), but the causes in FND-TG-501 (overuse, irrigation allocation, tariffs) are untouched, and demand and network losses are left to grow. Phase 3 could look at pairing each plant with dedicated solar or wind supply (SONEDE's mapped 78 MW solar potential is a lead, SRC-TG-279), sequencing desalination after network-loss reduction, and pricing that signals the cost of new water.
