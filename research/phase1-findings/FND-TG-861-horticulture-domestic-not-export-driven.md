## Problem

A common view holds that Tunisia drains its water by exporting thirsty fruit and vegetables such as tomatoes, watermelons, citrus and strawberries. The evidence does not support this for these crops. In 2022, official FAOSTAT figures show exports of 27,264 t of fresh tomatoes out of 1.16 million t produced, 18,333 t of watermelons out of 450,000 t, and 12,638 t of oranges out of 345,000 t (SRC-TG-862, SRC-TG-863). These are export shares of about 2.4%, 4.1% and 3.7% (our calculation). Strawberry exports were 688 t (SRC-TG-863). Processed tomatoes are the exception: 33,416 t of tomato paste were exported in 2022, more than the fresh tomato tonnage even before converting paste to fresh equivalent (SRC-TG-863), and tomato derivatives are named as an export product (SRC-TG-865). The fresh-equivalent share of tomato production exported as paste could not be computed. A French government brief states that fruit and vegetable production is mainly for domestic consumption, except citrus, dates and tomato and olive derived products (SRC-TG-865). The World Bank reports that domestic demand absorbs over 90% of citrus output (SRC-TG-710). The water used by irrigated fresh vegetables and citrus is therefore spent mainly on feeding the home market. Export-oriented water use is concentrated in dates and olive oil (FND-TG-860).

## Root causes

The symptom is heavy irrigation of vegetables and citrus in water-short areas. The causes are domestic food policy and irrigation policy, not export promotion.

- **Past decision: self-sufficiency through irrigation.** Irrigated area rose from about 65,000 ha (1956) to over 496,000 ha (2015). The national studies centre states that this made Tunisia self-sufficient in fruit and vegetables, with irrigated exports adding over 30% of agricultural exports (SRC-TG-864). Irrigated agriculture's share of agri-food exports was 24% in 1995, 33% in 2010 and 29% in 2014, and the same report calls this contribution "one of the major justifications" for irrigated agriculture (SRC-TG-864). Exports were thus part of the official case for irrigation. For fresh vegetables and citrus, however, the volumes exported remain small. The export value of irrigated agriculture is concentrated in dates (FND-TG-860); the per-crop breakdown in SRC-TG-864 is unusable.
- **Past decision: an anti-export bias.** Using 2000-2012 data, the World Bank (2014) found that policy distorted production "away from Mediterranean products" toward cereals, milk and beef. Non-competitive products made up 39% of production but about 52% of sector growth, and protection introduced "an anti-export bias" (SRC-TG-710). In 2008-2009, irrigation subsidies were about 18% of input subsidies (SRC-TG-710). This contradicts the idea that export incentives pushed farmers into water-intensive horticulture. The same source rates Tunisia as competitive in tomatoes, oranges and potatoes (SRC-TG-710).
- **Unused export access.** Tunisia used about 55% of its EU citrus quota in 1998 and 60-62% in 2010-2011, 25% of its tomato quota in 1998 and 52-71% in 2010-2011, and about 20% of its conditioned olive oil quota (SRC-TG-710).
- **Regional pattern.** In Cap Bon, private perimeters developed citrus and vegetables "at the expense of" groundwater overexploitation. Safeguard programmes then brought in northern water (SRC-TG-864). From the late 1980s the state launched a water mobilisation programme to safeguard irrigated agriculture, relying on five dams and the Medjerda-Cap Bon canal (SRC-TG-867). SONEDE's withdrawals from that canal have doubled since 1997, and the split has reversed in favour of drinking water (SRC-TG-867). In central Tunisia, the private irrigated sector is "dynamic" but overdraws shallow groundwater (SRC-TG-864).

## Evidence

- Production 2022 (official): tomatoes 1,160,000 t; watermelons 450,000 t; oranges 345,000 t (SRC-TG-862).
- Exports 2022: tomatoes 27,264 t; watermelons 18,333 t; oranges 12,638 t; strawberries 688 t; tomato paste 33,416 t of product. Exports 2023: tomatoes 25,434 t; watermelons 4,351 t; oranges 7,474 t; tomato paste 8,595 t (SRC-TG-863). Fresh tomato exports rose from 1,560 t in 2000 to 25,000-27,000 t in 2020-2023 (SRC-TG-863). They are growing fast, but from a low base.
- A peer-reviewed study agrees that tomatoes are "exported to a little extent" (SRC-TG-860). Vegetables' virtual water is about 60% blue (SRC-TG-861).
- Value per m3: irrigated vegetables returned 1.098 DT/m3 with the PNEEI programme and 1.234 DT/m3 without. Seasonal tomato and summer onion value water least among high-yield vegetables, while early pepper values it better (SRC-TG-864). Tomatoes have the highest economic blue water productivity of the main crops, at US$1.13/m3 (SRC-TG-860).
- **Conflicting evidence on strawberries.** A 2013 IRMC field note says Cap Bon strawberry area is growing fast and is "mainly destined for export", extending peak irrigation demand from May to September (SRC-TG-867). FAOSTAT records only 688 t of exports in 2022, against an estimated production of about 10,000 t that has been flat for 20 years (SRC-TG-862, SRC-TG-863). The FAOSTAT production series is estimated or imputed, so the conflict cannot be settled here.
- **Conflicting evidence on EU tomato access.** One source gives an 18,816 t EU tomato quota for 2010-2011 (SRC-TG-710). Another gives unlimited duty-free access from 1 October to 31 May (SRC-TG-865).

## Why confidence is what it is

Medium. The national export shares rest on official FAOSTAT production and trade figures (tier A), but the shares are our own calculation and cover calendar years. The policy diagnosis rests on one World Bank chapter with 2000-2012 data (tier B), and no current legal text on export incentives was opened, so the anti-export bias is documented only up to about 2012, confirmed in part by a French government brief (tier B). The finding is weakest at regional level. No governorate data for Sidi Bouzid or Kairouan was opened, and the Cap Bon evidence is a 2013 research note plus a national typology. Off-season and greenhouse exports could not be separated, and these may be more export-oriented than the national totals suggest.

The finding refutes the framing only for fresh vegetables, watermelons, citrus and strawberries at national scale. It does not refute it for dates and olive oil (FND-TG-860).

## Links to other sectors

- **Water:** horticulture in Cap Bon and central Tunisia relies on overdrawn groundwater and, in Cap Bon, on water transferred through the Medjerda-Cap Bon canal. That transferred water is increasingly diverted to drinking water (SRC-TG-864, SRC-TG-867). The aquifer and transfer issues belong to the water lane.
- **Water allocation outlook:** the agricultural water allocation is planned to fall from 2,140 Mm3 (2010) to 2,035 Mm3 (2030), with per-hectare allocation down from about 4,700 to 4,050 m3 (SRC-TG-864). The crops that would absorb this cut are not specified.
- **Energy:** none found.

## Leads for phase 2

- Programme national d'économie d'eau en irrigation (PNEEI) and its 2016 evaluation by SCET Tunisie (cited in SRC-TG-864).
- Programmes de sauvegarde des périmètres privés du Cap Bon using northern water transfers, and the Medjerda-Cap Bon canal managed by SECADENORD (SRC-TG-864, SRC-TG-867).
- Reorienting support from continental to Mediterranean products, and horizontal (non crop-specific) policies, as proposed by the World Bank (SRC-TG-710).
- Better use of EU preferential quotas for citrus and tomatoes, and harvest and post-harvest quality improvements for citrus (SRC-TG-710).
- Zoning crops by virtual water content and bioclimatic area (SRC-TG-861).
