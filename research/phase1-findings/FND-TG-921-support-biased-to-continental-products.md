## Problem

Tunisian agricultural policy works mainly through guaranteed producer prices for cereals, intervention buying by state offices for milk, sugar beet and tobacco, high tariffs on agri-food imports and fixed consumer prices and margins (SRC-TG-710, SRC-TG-924). The World Bank calls this "a food security policy" rather than an agricultural policy. In its view the policy pulls land, capital and labour towards continental products (cereals, milk, beef), where Tunisia is not competitive, and away from Mediterranean products (olive oil, fruit, vegetables, dried fruit), where it has a comparative advantage (SRC-TG-710). The direct budget cost was about TND 350 million, or 0.8% of GDP, in 2010 (the report text says 2010, while Table 9.3 gives 350.6 for 2009 and has no 2010 column). Between 2000 and 2009 the share of market price support and input subsidies in agricultural transfers rose from 31% to 53%, while investment aid and general services fell (SRC-TG-710). The support is regressive: it is based on output and farm size, so it mostly benefits large wheat, milk and beef producers. Olive, fruit and vegetable growers in the interior get little support and have difficulty getting finance (SRC-TG-710).

**Correction to the framing we were given.** The research brief asked whether administered prices impose negative support on farmers. The evidence says the sign has changed over time and, for cereals, varies with world prices. Before 1986, prices were fixed below world levels and agriculture was implicitly taxed (SRC-TG-920). From 1987, cereal producer prices were pushed above world prices (NPC 1.37 for durum in late 1987) (SRC-TG-920), and wheat remained "overprotected" in 1995 (SRC-TG-921). The 2014 analysis, using 2000s data, describes support, not taxation, for cereals, milk and beef (SRC-TG-710). After the 2021-2022 world price rise the sign flipped again for cereals. The World Bank wrote in 2024 that decreed farmgate prices "have been below import-parity prices". Even after increases of about 61-64% between 2021 and 2023, February 2024 farmgate prices for durum wheat and barley were 3% and 14% below the OC import cost (SRC-TG-705). Because the decreed price is fixed for a season while world prices move, cereal growers are subsidised in some years and implicitly taxed in others (see FND-TG-702). Over the 2000s, when evidence exists across products, the problem was mainly how support was distributed (by product, farm size and region) and how it was delivered (price support instead of investment and services), not a net tax on farmers. Whether that still holds after 2021 cannot be shown without a PSE/NRA series. Mediterranean products are disadvantaged relatively, because protection pulls resources into continental products (SRC-TG-710). This record found no sourced estimate of whether Mediterranean products are taxed in absolute terms (see data_gaps).

## Root causes

1. **Self-sufficiency in staples as the policy goal (legacy).** The state cereal system set up in the 1960s-70s is covered in FND-TG-702. The PASA reforms of 1986-1995 cut input subsidies but kept administered wheat prices and the offices' marketing role (FND-TG-920, SRC-TG-921). The guaranteed-price and state-trading structure in place in 2016 is the result (SRC-TG-924).
2. **Output-based and area-based support.** Because support is based on output and farm size, it goes mostly to a few large landowners. The World Bank says this contradicts the common belief in Tunisia that the policy protects small family farms (SRC-TG-710).
3. **State trading and price controls.** The Office des Céréales held the wheat import monopoly until 2016 under the WTO account; the World Bank later described a de jure grain monopoly except barley (SRC-TG-924, SRC-TG-705). The OCT imported sugar in a de facto monopoly according to Tunisia's 2006 state-trading notification (SRC-TG-924, para. 3.168). The state fixed consumer prices for bread, flour, couscous, oil, sugar, milk and tea, and margins for rice, poultry, eggs and many fruits and vegetables (SRC-TG-924). The ONH guarantees a minimum price to olive oil producers who cannot sell (SRC-TG-924).
4. **Drift back towards distorting instruments.** Tunisia committed at the Uruguay Round (1994) to reduce domestic support from USD 76 million to 66 million, yet agricultural support spending rose steadily over the 2000s (SRC-TG-710). After PASA removed fertiliser and feed subsidies (FND-TG-920), input subsidies, mainly fuel, milk collection premia and irrigation, made up 20-25% of transfers by 2008-2009 (SRC-TG-710).

## Evidence

- Market price support is over 30% of budget transfers. Milk takes over 50% of price support spending and cereals about a third (SRC-TG-710, p. 265).
- Input subsidies are 20-25% of transfers. In 2008-09 they went about 40% to fuel, 40% to milk collection premia and 18% to irrigation (SRC-TG-710, p. 266).
- Average MFN tariff on agri-food is 24.6%, against 16.5% for all products. 60.5% of agricultural tariff lines exceed 15% (SRC-TG-710, p. 266).
- Budget cost was reported as TND 350 million (0.8% of GDP) in 2010; Table 9.3 gives TND 350.6 million for 2009 and has no 2010 column. The share of price support and input subsidies rose from 31% (2000) to 53% (2009) (SRC-TG-710, p. 266).
- Agricultural output grew 67% in value over 1990-2010. The source says "almost one-third (17 percent of the value)" came from beef and milk; 17/67 is about 25%, so the denominator is ambiguous (SRC-TG-710, p. 265).
- Consumer subsidies for cereals rose from TND 170 million (2004) to TND 1,360 million (2014) according to the WTO text (SRC-TG-924, para. 4.34). The same report's Table 3.20 gives cereals and derivatives at TND 170.9 million (2005), 1,152.4 million (2014) and 1,359.3 million (2015), so the source is internally inconsistent on the 2014 value; roughly TND 1.2-1.4 billion in 2014-15.
- Model estimates from World Bank (2006), cited in 2014. Agricultural trade protection costs consumers 4-5.6% of spending. Removing tariffs would raise GDP by about 0.8% but shift about 87,000 agricultural jobs to other sectors. About 70% of farms would gain from removing price distortions, mostly olive, fruit, vegetable and sheep farms in the centre and south. Losers would be cereal farms in the north and north-west (SRC-TG-710, pp. 267-268). These are counterfactual simulations, not measurements.

**Counter-view.** Boughzala and Elloumi (1992) found, with a risk-constrained farm model, that full liberalisation was not in Tunisia's interest while the EEC protected and subsidised its own agriculture. They found Tunisia would gain only if major producers also liberalised (SRC-TG-922). The 2014 World Bank simulation includes a scenario without EU subsidies that still shows gains (SRC-TG-710), but the two studies use different models and periods. This record does not settle the disagreement.

**Source conflict.** The WTO Secretariat, citing Tunisia's 2006 state-trading notification, lists the Office des Céréales as wheat and barley import monopolist. In its 2016 update it says the OC held the wheat import monopoly until 2016, with wheat duties cut to zero from January 2016 (SRC-TG-924). The World Bank in 2024 describes the OC as holding a de jure monopoly over grain trade, domestic collection and imports, except barley, opened to selected private operators for imports since April 2023 (SRC-TG-705). All three statements are recorded.

## Why confidence is what it is

Medium. The description of instruments (administered prices, state monopolies, price controls) is consistent across the World Bank and the WTO Secretariat (SRC-TG-710, SRC-TG-924). The diagnosis of bias and regressivity rests mainly on one institution, the World Bank, which has advocated liberalisation since 1986. Its quantitative impacts are model simulations from a 2006 study we could not open. Budget data stop at 2010 and WTO data at 2016, so whether the structure still holds in 2026 is assumed, not shown (trend: unknown). Apart from the 2024 cereal price comparison (SRC-TG-705), no PSE/NRA series was found to quantify support by product (see data_gaps).

## Links to other sectors

- **Energy.** Fuel subsidies were about 40% of agricultural input subsidies in 2008-09 (SRC-TG-710). Agricultural support therefore depends partly on energy pricing policy (energy lane, theme energy-subsidies).
- **Water.** Irrigation subsidies were about 18% of input subsidies in 2008-09 (SRC-TG-710). Protection of irrigated milk and beef production shapes demand for irrigation water. This record does not quantify the water used by protected products.
- **Agriculture (other findings).** Cereal-specific pricing and the subsidy system are in FND-TG-702, import dependence in FND-TG-700. Lack of finance for Mediterranean crops is in FND-TG-922.

## Leads for phase 2

- Shift from price support to decoupled direct income payments, as proposed by the World Bank (SRC-TG-710, recommendations section; compared to the EU CAP reforms since the mid-1990s).
- Horizontal support (finance, risk management, extension, land market) instead of product-specific support (SRC-TG-710).
- Fuller use of EU preferential export quotas for olive oil, citrus and tomatoes (SRC-TG-710).
- Zero wheat import duty from January 2016 (SRC-TG-924, para. 4.35).
- Investment incentives code and Decree n° 94-427 of 14 February 1994 on agricultural investment grants (SRC-TG-924, para. 4.13).
- ONH minimum price guarantee for olive oil (SRC-TG-924, para. 4.48).
