## Problem

Fresh milk production peaked at about 1,462 thousand t in 2021 and fell to 1,230 thousand t in 2025, about 16% lower. Over 2016-2025 the average change was -1.5% a year (SRC-TG-761). The dairy herd was estimated at 360,000 female units in 2024 and has shrunk 2.5% a year since 2015. No livestock census has been run since 2019, so recent herd numbers are estimates (SRC-TG-761). Yield per cow also fell, from about 6,279 kg in 2015 to 5,749 kg in 2024 (SRC-TG-761). Dairy imports grew 21.1% a year in value over 2016-2025, reaching 292.1 million TND in 2025, for a dairy trade deficit of 233.4 million TND (SRC-TG-761). This is the end of a long expansion: production rose from 400 million litres in 1990 to 870 million litres in 2000 under a deliberate policy (SRC-TG-760).

## Root causes

**Past decision (legacy).** After a phase of reconstituting milk from imported powder, Tunisia switched in the 1980s-1990s to promoting local milk. From 1983 collection centres received a premium of 20% of the producer price. The take-off of the early 1990s combined several measures: a surtax on imported milk powder, an extended consumer milk subsidy, a higher producer price, facilities to import dairy cows, a "guaranteed regular supply of concentrate feed, mainly through imports", and fodder crop support (SRC-TG-760). The resulting herd is 59% pure-bred Holstein (SRC-TG-761), a breed that depends on concentrates and good forage. Part of the herd growth came from cattle units set up in unsuitable places, such as oases and urban belts, run "landless" with below-standard performance (SRC-TG-760). Production grew from 400 to 730 million litres between 1990 and 1998 (SRC-TG-760). The model was exposed from the start: production fell in the 2001-2002 droughts (SRC-TG-760). Border protection for milk was cut from a nominal rate of 55.8% in 1995 to 3.6% in 2000 (SRC-TG-760). Earlier, the 1989-1995 adjustment programme had already committed to aligning milk prices more closely with world prices and to liberalising milk imports (SRC-TG-921). Price support for milk did not disappear, though. In the late 2000s milk took over half of market price support spending, and milk collection premia were about 40% of input subsidies in 2008-2009 (SRC-TG-710; see FND-TG-921). Milk was a supported product in the 2000s; the current complaint is that the administered price has lagged costs since 2022.

**Administered price against rising costs.** The state sets the minimum farm-gate milk price. It was raised in steps from 736 millimes per litre (January 2015) to 1,340 (SRC-TG-761) and has not moved since late 2022 or early 2023. Over 2019-2022 the sourced numbers do not show a squeeze on their own. The price rose from 945 (October 2019) to 1,340 (November 2022), about +42%, while concentrate prices rose 35% between 2019 and 2022 (SRC-TG-706). The gap shows during 2021-2022, when the price stood at 1,140 (+21% over 2019) as feed costs climbed. It shows again after the freeze, for which no sourced feed cost series was found. The ministry itself describes "a lasting imbalance between production costs and selling prices". It attributes this to the world surge in feed raw material prices and to droughts that cut roughage output and increased dependence on compound feed (SRC-TG-766). UTAP claimed a production cost of 1.85 TND per litre in May 2023 against 1.34 TND paid, a loss of about 500 millimes per litre (SRC-TG-765; tier C, interested party). ITES reports that some poultry and dairy farmers partly or fully abandoned production after the input price rises (SRC-TG-706).

**Symptom vs cause.** Milk shortages and falling collection are symptoms. The causes are a feed-intensive, import-dependent production model (FND-TG-760), an administered output price that lags input costs, and recurrent drought that removes the cheaper home-grown roughage.

## Evidence

- Production 2016-2025, read from chart (thousand t): 1408, 1424, 1310, 1352, 1392, 1462, 1330, 1224, 1248, 1230 (SRC-TG-761). The 2025 value and the -1.5% average are confirmed in the table on the same page.
- Herd 360,000 female units in 2024 (211,000 Holstein, 149,000 local and crossbred), -2.5%/yr over 2015-2024. Note: "Le recensement n'a pas été réalisé depuis 2019" (SRC-TG-761).
- A cow with calf cost 7,056 TND in 2024, rising 9.5% a year since 2015 (SRC-TG-761). Rebuilding the herd is therefore expensive.
- Farm-gate milk price path (OEP via ONAGRI), in millimes per litre: 736 (Jan 2015), 766 (Apr 2017), 890 (Jul 2018), 945 (Oct 2019), 1,040 (Jul 2020), 1,140 (Apr 2021), 1,340 (Nov 2022), unchanged in 2024 (SRC-TG-761).
- **Sources disagree on the dates.** ONAGRI gives 1,040 millimes in July 2020 and the last revision in November 2022 (SRC-TG-761). The ministry's answer to parliament, as reported by the press, says the price went from 945 to 1,340 millimes "between August 2020 and February 2023" in three revisions, with the collection premium raised to 115 millimes per litre (SRC-TG-766). Both agree on the current level of 1,340.
- Dairy trade 2025: imports 29.4 thousand t (292.1 million TND) and exports 9.3 thousand t (58.7 million TND). ONAGRI's import penetration is 2.3% of production, but it compares tonnes of dairy products (powder, butter, cheese) with tonnes of fresh milk, not milk equivalent, so it understates dependence. Import value is growing fast (SRC-TG-761).
- In 2022 a fall in barley imports left feed temporarily short, which "affected the availability of milk" and led to "light shortages for a few weeks" (SRC-TG-706).
- Historical: milk production was 140 million litres in 1970, 400 in 1990 and 870 in 2000 (SRC-TG-760).
- **Herd, yield and production do not reconcile in the ONAGRI fiche.** 360,000 cows at 5,749 kg a year would give about 2.07 million t, against about 1.25 million t produced in 2024 (SRC-TG-761). The female-unit herd probably includes non-lactating animals, or the yield refers to controlled herds only, but the fiche does not say. Herd and yield trends are therefore treated as indicative.

## Why confidence is what it is

Medium overall, but split. The contraction of production is solid (high). The herd decline is medium, because there has been no census since 2019 and herd and yield do not reconcile. The size of the price-cost gap after 2022 is low, because it rests on the ministry's description and a UTAP figure. Production, price and trade series come from ONAGRI's official fiche (tier A). Year-by-year chart values are read from labels, but the endpoints match the printed table. Herd figures after 2019 are estimates without a census, which weakens the herd-decline indicator. The cost side is the weak point. The only opened production cost figure is a UTAP statement (tier C), so the size of the price-cost gap is not established by an independent source. The direction of the squeeze is supported by ITES (concentrate +35%, tier B) and by the ministry's own description (tier C press report). The legacy policy account rests on one peer-reviewed synthesis (SRC-TG-760) citing an IAMM evaluation (Bourbouze and Elloumi, 1998) that was not opened.

The brief framed this as a "milk shortage crisis in 2022-2023". The evidence supports a sustained contraction of production and herd, with short shortages in 2022. It does not show a collapse of supply. Fresh milk output in 2025 was about 1.23 million t, about 13% below its 2016 level of 1.41 million t (SRC-TG-761): a decline, not a collapse.

## Links to other sectors

- **Water:** the ministry links the crisis to recurrent droughts and water scarcity that cut roughage production (SRC-TG-766). Part of the Holstein herd was installed in oases and peri-urban "landless" units (SRC-TG-760), where forage for cattle competes for scarce irrigation water. The water lane covers oasis aquifers; no source here quantifies water use by dairy forage.
- **Energy:** none found. (Milk cooling and collection use electricity, but no source was found on this as a constraint.)
