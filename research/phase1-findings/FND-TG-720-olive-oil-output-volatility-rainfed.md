## Problem

Olive oil is Tunisia's main agricultural export: TND 4.1 billion in 2025, 2.4% of GDP and 6% of total exports (INS, cited in SRC-TG-724). About 310,000 farmers and nearly 1 million people depend on it (ONH, cited in SRC-TG-724). Yet one season's output is a poor guide to the next. The IOC series runs from 35 kt (2001/02) to 440 kt (2019/20). Its coefficient of variation is about 48% over 1990/91-2024/25 and about 47% over 2010/11-2024/25, our calculation (SRC-TG-720). Across the 14 year-to-year transitions ending with 2024/25, 9 absolute changes exceeded 50%, also our calculation from SRC-TG-720.

Recent swings: from 440 kt (2019/20) to 140 kt (2020/21) (SRC-TG-720), then from 220 kt (2023/24, final) to 340 kt (2024/25, provisional) and an estimated 450 kt (2025/26) (SRC-TG-721). The 2022/23 season looked like a drop to 180 kt in the provisional data (SRC-TG-722). On final data it was 217 kt, close to the 240 kt of 2021/22 (SRC-TG-723, SRC-TG-722). Price moves follow the volume swings. Average export prices halved from 24.5 TND/kg in 2024 to 12.8 TND/kg in 2025: export volumes rose 60% but export value fell 16% (SRC-TG-724). On the domestic market the price per litre fell from 18 TND at the start of the 2024/25 harvest to about 14 TND by mid-December, and to 12-13 TND in Kasserine (tier C, SRC-TG-731). Trésor describes the volatility as causing "une importante cyclicité des prix" (a strong price cycle) (SRC-TG-724).

## Root causes

**Symptom vs cause.** The source set documents high production variability, rainfed groves concentrated in dry areas and historically low yields; it does not isolate a national causal contribution from rainfall versus alternate bearing and other factors.

1. **Rainfed groves on dry land (legacy).** FAO reports about 3% irrigated and 85% of groves in the drier Centre and South (SRC-TG-726). It separately gives an average of 60,000 irrigated ha, 3.5% of area, for 2010-2012 (SRC-TG-726); the summary estimates differ. Chemlali, which gives 80% of national oil, is cultivated in the Centre and South where rainfall is under 250 mm/year (SRC-TG-726). Recent figures for the irrigated share differ: about 5%, or about 100,000 ha, per the ONH CEO in 2020 (tier C, SRC-TG-730), and 6% per ONH as cited in 2026 (SRC-TG-724). The extensive model, rows 17-24 m apart and 80-120 trees/ha, was set up on the Sfax plain under the French protectorate, partly in relation to trade links and customs policies favouring France (SRC-TG-725).
2. **Low yields, declining over about 1990-2012.** FAO (2015) ranks Tunisia last of nine EU/MENA producers for yield per hectare. Yields fell 0.4%/yr on average after 1990, to 0.47 t olives/ha in the four years before the report, against 0.63 t/ha in 1990-1994 (SRC-TG-726). Named causes are low density, poorly maintained and ageing trees, poor agronomic practice, little mechanisation and limited plant protection (SRC-TG-726). On tree age, the report gives an age structure with 40% of plantations over 40 years, and an expert estimate that up to 25% are over 75 years. Its summary simplifies this to "25% over 70 years" (SRC-TG-726). Local Chemlali trees on small Sfax farms are "often old (70 years)" (SRC-TG-725).
3. **Fragmented smallholdings with little capital.** Farms under 5 ha are almost half of olive farms (FAO 2015, cited in SRC-TG-725). The fragmented structure leads to chronic under-investment in irrigation, equipment and grove renewal (SRC-TG-724). A Chemlali replant takes about five years to bear, which families with little other income cannot afford (SRC-TG-725). There are few producer cooperatives (SRC-TG-726), and Lavie et al. name the lack of a cooperative model as an obstacle (SRC-TG-725). See FND-TG-780 on farm fragmentation in general.
4. **Alternate bearing.** On the Sfax plain "an olive tree only produces fruit every 3 years on average" (SRC-TG-725). This adds a biological cycle on top of rainfall.

**Contrary evidence.** Area and output have grown over time. Area for oil rose from about 1.5 Mha (1990) to 1.8 Mha (2011) (SRC-TG-726), and about 2 Mha are cited today (SRC-TG-724). Trésor describes a "croissance tendancielle" (upward trend) in production and exports since 2011 (SRC-TG-724). Volatility is the persistent problem, not a long-run fall in volume. The growth may come more from area than yield, though no current yield figure was found.

**The response is irrigated intensification.** Since the 2010s, and faster after the 2017/18 price crisis, large groups and some families have planted intensive (200 trees/ha) and hyper-intensive (over 1,000 trees/ha) irrigated groves (SRC-TG-725). Chemlali does not tolerate intensive cultivation, so these groves use Spanish cultivars (Arbequina, Arbosana). Some of these farms are on leased state land (SRC-TG-725). The ONH target was to raise oil from irrigated groves from 80 kt to 100 kt by 2025 (SRC-TG-725, SRC-TG-730). The hyper-intensive system, introduced in 2000, yields 8.5 t olives/ha against 0.7 t on average for rainfed groves (SRC-TG-726). Irrigated systems may reduce exposure to rainfall for adopters, but this effect was not quantified in the sources opened; they also draw on groundwater (see Links).

## Evidence

- IOC production series 1990/91-2024/25, Tunisia row (SRC-TG-720). Recent seasons: 2019/20 440 kt, 2020/21 140, 2021/22 240, 2022/23 217, 2023/24 220 (provisional in that vintage), 2024/25 340 (forecast in that vintage).
- **Source disagreement on 2022/23.** IOC provisional (Nov 2023): 180 kt (SRC-TG-722). IOC final (Nov 2024): 217 kt (SRC-TG-723, SRC-TG-720). 180 kt is the figure most quoted in Tunisian media.
- 2024/25 is 340 kt (provisional) and 2025/26 an estimated 450 kt, above the 2019/20 record (SRC-TG-721).
- Export prices: 24.5 TND/kg (2024) and 12.8 TND/kg (2025), from ONAGRI data cited by SRC-TG-724.
- FAOSTAT olive (fruit) production: 1,200,000 t in 2022, official (SRC-TG-862). Not used for yields because the area series is imputed.
- **Source disagreement on irrigated area.** 60,000 ha (3.5%, 2010-2012) (SRC-TG-726); about 100,000 ha (about 5%, 2020, tier C) (SRC-TG-730); 6% (SRC-TG-724). The denominators also differ (1.7-2 Mha of olive area).

## Why confidence is what it is

Medium. The national production series and computed volatility are supported by the IOC dataset (tier A). The explanation for volatility is less certain: the quantitative yield diagnosis is from a 2015 FAO/EBRD report (tier B) with data to about 2013, and the peer-reviewed study is local to Sfax and Sidi Bouzid. The source set describes rainfall dependence but does not quantify the rainfall-production relationship nationally. Domestic price evidence is tier C only.

## Links to other sectors

- **Water.** The main response to volatility, irrigated intensification, relies on groundwater. In the Sfax and Sidi Bouzid study area, deep aquifers are described as "very expensive and overexploited" and shallow groundwater as "largely overexploited and saline", and more than half of wells are illegal (SRC-TG-725). Liberalised access to groundwater dates from the 1986 structural adjustment programme (SRC-TG-725). Aquifer balances belong to the water lane (theme groundwater) and are not quantified here. See FND-TG-802 on salinisation of irrigated soils.
- **Macro and trade.** Olive oil swings move 6% of total exports (2025, INS via SRC-TG-724).

## Verification

The IOC seasonal series establishes output volatility. Rainfed cultivation and alternate bearing are documented features of production, but the sources do not quantify the share of national variation explained by either. No causal attribution coefficient is claimed (SRC-TG-720, SRC-TG-726).

**Outstanding verification:** The national production series is verified. The study evidence does not isolate the separate contributions of rainfall exposure, alternate bearing and management.
