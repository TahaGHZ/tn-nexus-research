## Problem

From the 1970s Tunisia relied on universal, untargeted energy and food subsidies as a central
part of social protection. The World Bank working paper says they became its backbone because the
informal sector was large, poverty high, and information systems and registries to identify poor
households were lacking; it says energy subsidies had not been reformed in depth since then
(SRC-TG-162). Total subsidies (energy, food and transport) rose from 2% of GDP in 2005 to 7% in
2013. The paper reports that energy subsidies increased fourfold over that period and rose from
about one-third of total subsidies before the revolution to about two-thirds in 2013; its figure
uses Ministry of Finance data from October 2013 and marks the November 2013 energy value as a
forecast (SRC-TG-162). The distinct World Bank policy note estimates total energy subsidies at
4.7% of GDP in April 2013, composed of 3.3% direct price subsidies and 1.4% allocated to refinery
and power production. It separately describes end-2013 plans of 4.7% direct subsidies and 6.6%
including implicit subsidies; neither is an outturn (SRC-TG-172). A separate IMF gross fiscal
series reports 4,434 million TND (5.9% of GDP) in 2013, 2,689 million TND (3.8%) in 2012,
3,112 million TND (3.9%) in 2014 and an estimated 918 million TND (1.1%) in 2015. This series is
gross and differs in definition from the World Bank estimates (SRC-TG-163).

The distributional evidence concerns absolute benefits and consumption. A World Bank 2014
estimate says the bottom 40% captured 29% of energy subsidy benefits, but its underlying
observation year is not stated. A separate model using 2010 household survey data estimates that
the poorest quintile received 14.9% of residential energy subsidy benefits and the richest
quintile 25%; this does not cover industry or firms' electricity use. The World Bank's 2013 policy note, using the 2010 household survey with fuel prices adjusted to April 2013, gives a third basis for all energy products: 13% of benefits to the lowest-income quintile and 29% to the highest (SRC-TG-172, Figure 11). The paper also reports that
a quintile-5 individual consumed 200 times as much gasoline as one in the poorest quintile. These
findings concern absolute benefits and consumption, not subsidy burden as a share of household
budgets (SRC-TG-162).

## Root causes

- **Legacy decision (1970s):** universal subsidies were chosen because the informal sector was
  large, poverty high and there were no information systems or registries to identify and target
  poor households (SRC-TG-162). This is the root decision. Rising budget cost is a symptom.
- **Partial pass-through:** domestic prices were adjusted only partly to international oil
  prices, so the subsidy bill rose as international prices increased; the World Bank paper
  attributes sustained increases since 2010 to partial rather than full pass-through
  (SRC-TG-162).
- **Product-based design:** subsidies follow consumption, so richer households that consume more
  gasoline, diesel and electricity receive more in absolute terms. The paper reports that an
  individual in the richest consumption quintile used about 200 times more gasoline than an
  individual in the poorest quintile (SRC-TG-162).
- **Regulated prices that did not fully reflect costs (documented later):** the IMF's 2021
  Article IV report says major SOEs suffered structural losses because regulated prices did not
  align with incurred costs (SRC-TG-164). This supports a later, general cost-pricing mechanism;
  it does not establish that this mechanism caused the 2013 aggregate SOE deficit or that budget
  transfers, rather than price changes, absorbed that deficit. Separately, the IMF's 2016 report
  records a consolidated deficit of 4.4% of GDP for all non-financial SOEs in 2013, with STEG
  among several leading loss-makers, and transfers to public enterprises overall rising from
  about 2% of GDP in 2010 to about 7% in 2013 (SRC-TG-163). These aggregates are contextual
  fiscal outcomes, not a STEG-specific deficit or proof of a single causal pathway.

**Who decides:** fuel prices are set administratively. Under the 2013-2015 IMF Stand-By
Arrangement, a Ministry of Industry decree approving a new automatic fuel pricing formula was a
structural benchmark marked completed (SRC-TG-163, Annex Table 1); by timing it is the January
2014 gasoline formula (SRC-TG-162). The 2016 symmetric formula was a government commitment in
the MEFP (SRC-TG-163); the act adopting it was not found. Who sets electricity tariffs is not
sourced here (see data gaps). The common description of energy subsidies as run by the Caisse
Générale de Compensation is not supported by the documents opened: the IMF fiscal table labels
the CGC line "CGC (Food)" and lists energy subsidies on a separate line (SRC-TG-163). This
classification does not establish the payment channel for energy subsidies before 2015; that
question remains open.

**Reform attempts, 2012-2016:** prices of gasoline, diesel and electricity rose 7% in September
2012 and again in March 2013; cement subsidies were removed in 2014; low and medium voltage
tariffs rose 10% in January and 10% in May 2014; a lifeline tariff for households under 100 kWh
per month was introduced in 2014; an automatic gasoline formula was set in January 2014 without
a smoothing mechanism or calendar (SRC-TG-162). Under the 2016 IMF programme the government
committed to a symmetric automatic formula for gasoline and diesel, adopted in May 2016,
quarterly from July 2016 and monthly from January 2017, followed by electricity tariff increases
towards cost recovery with a lifeline tariff (SRC-TG-163). How that mechanism fared later is in
FND-TG-161.

## Evidence

- Total subsidies (energy, food and transport) 2% (2005) to 7% (2013); energy subsidies
  increased fourfold and their share of total subsidies shifted from about one-third before the
  revolution to about two-thirds in 2013. The November 2013 endpoint in Figure 1 is a forecast;
  the figure cites Ministry of Finance data from October 2013 (SRC-TG-162, pp. 4-6, Figure 1).
- 2013 energy-subsidy estimates use different definitions. World Bank WPS 7312 gives a November
  2013 forecast of 4.7% of GDP with ambiguous composition (SRC-TG-162). The separate World Bank
  policy note reports an April 2013 estimate of 4.7% total, comprising 3.3% direct price
  subsidies plus 1.4% for refinery and power production; it separately describes 4.7% direct
  planned for end-2013 and 6.6% including 1.9% implicit subsidies (SRC-TG-172, PDF pp. 9, 17).
  The IMF gross series gives 4,434 million TND or 5.9% of GDP in 2013, 2,689 million TND or
  3.8% in 2012, 3,112 million TND or 3.9% in 2014, and estimated 918 million TND or 1.1% in
  2015 (SRC-TG-163, Tables 4a-4b). These are distinct estimates/series, not a common outturn.
- Incidence: the World Bank paper (WPS 7312, June 2015) attributes a 29% bottom-40 share to World Bank 2014, but the
  observation year is unstated (SRC-TG-162, p. 2). Its separate Table 7 model, based on 2010
  household survey data, estimates that the poorest quintile received 14.9% and the richest 25%
  of residential energy-subsidy benefits; this is not an economy-wide estimate (SRC-TG-162). The 2013 World Bank policy note's Figure 11 gives 13% (lowest quintile) and 29% (highest quintile) of all energy-subsidy benefits on the 2010 survey with April 2013 fuel prices; it is a different basis from both figures above and is not reconciled with them (SRC-TG-172).
- Consolidated non-financial-SOE deficit 4.4% of GDP in 2013, with STEG among several leading
  loss-makers; transfers to public enterprises overall rose from about 2% of GDP in 2010 to
  about 7% in 2013. These are distinct aggregates (SRC-TG-163, p. 25).
- Automatic fuel formula: the completed Ministry of Industry decree benchmark belongs to the
  earlier 2013-2015 SBA, corresponding by timing to the January 2014 formula. The 2016
  symmetric formula and staged schedule are stated as government commitments in the later EFF
  MEFP (SRC-TG-163, pp. 18, 54, 75; SRC-TG-162, p. 5).

## Why confidence is what it is

Medium. The historical rationale, subsidy trend and incidence rest mainly on one World Bank
working paper (tier B, not peer-reviewed), drawing on Ministry of Finance data and other
institutional sources. Its figure marks the November 2013 energy value as a forecast. The
separate World Bank policy note confirms that its 4.7% April 2013 estimate is 3.3% direct plus
1.4% allocated to refinery and power production, while end-2013 direct subsidies were planned at
4.7% and 6.6% including implicit support (SRC-TG-172). The IMF's later gross series gives
5.9% (SRC-TG-163); none of these is presented here as an official Ministry outturn on a single
stated definition.

The distributional estimates use different bases: the bottom-40 estimate has no stated
observation year, while the 14.9%/25% comparison is a model of residential consumer benefits
using 2010 survey data. The World Bank describes absolute energy-subsidy benefits as pro-rich
and regressive per capita. The poorest and richest quintiles each spend just over 6% of total
budgets on energy, but their source mix differs (electricity for the poorest, gasoline for the
richest) (SRC-TG-162). A 2019 ESCWA study reportedly reaches a contrary distributional result
but has not been opened; this finding therefore does not claim a settled overall incidence
conclusion.

## Links to other sectors

Not established with sources here. The same paper models food subsidies alongside energy
(SRC-TG-162), and diesel and electricity subsidies reach irrigation and agriculture, but no source
opened quantifies the agricultural share of energy subsidies. Listed as a lead, not a link.
