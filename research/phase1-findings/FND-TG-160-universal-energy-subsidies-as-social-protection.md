## Problem

From the 1970s Tunisia relied on universal, untargeted energy and food subsidies as its main
social protection tool (SRC-TG-162). Energy subsidies were not reformed in depth for four decades.
Their cost rose sharply when oil prices climbed. Total subsidies went from 2% of GDP in 2005 to 7%
in 2013, and energy subsidies rose about fourfold to 4.7% of GDP in 2013 (a forecast), becoming
about two-thirds of all subsidies (SRC-TG-162). The benefits went disproportionately to richer
households: the bottom 40% received 29% of energy subsidies, and in a simulation on 2010 survey
data the poorest fifth received 14.9% of residential energy subsidies against 25% for the richest
fifth (SRC-TG-162). At the same time, STEG led the loss-making state enterprises (SRC-TG-163).

## Root causes

- **Legacy decision (1970s):** universal subsidies were chosen because the informal sector was
  large, poverty high and no registry existed to target the poor (SRC-TG-162). This is the root
  decision. Rising budget cost is the symptom.
- **Partial pass-through:** domestic prices were adjusted only partly to international oil
  prices, so the budget absorbed the difference, with sustained increases after 2010 (SRC-TG-162).
- **Product-based design:** subsidies follow consumption, so richer households who consume much
  more gasoline, diesel and electricity receive more; a richest-quintile individual consumed about
  200 times more gasoline than one in the poorest quintile (SRC-TG-162).
- **Loss-making SOEs covered by transfers:** non-financial SOEs ran a deficit of 4.4% of GDP in 2013,
  three times 2010, led by STEG, and transfers to public enterprises rose from about 2% to about 7%
  of GDP between 2010 and 2013 (SRC-TG-163).

**Who decides:** fuel prices are set administratively; the 2016 automatic formula was to be
approved by a Ministry of Industry decree (SRC-TG-163). Who sets electricity tariffs is not sourced
here (see data gaps). Today the hydrocarbon subsidy line is split between STIR and STEG in budget
execution reports (SRC-TG-161), consistent with direct grants to the two companies. The common description of energy subsidies as run by
the Caisse Générale de Compensation is not supported by the documents opened: the IMF fiscal
table labels the CGC line "CGC (Food)" and lists energy subsidies on a separate line (SRC-TG-163).

**Reform attempts, 2012-2016:** prices of gasoline, diesel and electricity rose 7% in September
2012 and again in March 2013; cement subsidies were removed in 2014; low and medium voltage
tariffs rose 10% in January and 10% in May 2014; a lifeline tariff for households under 100 kWh
per month was introduced in 2014; an automatic gasoline formula was set in January 2014 without a
smoothing mechanism or calendar (SRC-TG-162). Under the 2016 IMF programme the government
committed to a symmetric automatic formula for gasoline and diesel, adopted in May 2016, quarterly
from July 2016 and monthly from January 2017, followed by electricity tariff increases towards
cost recovery with a lifeline tariff (SRC-TG-163). How that mechanism fared later is in FND-TG-161.

## Evidence

- Energy subsidies 4.7% of GDP in 2013; total subsidies 2% (2005) to 7% (2013); energy share from
  one-third to about two-thirds (SRC-TG-162, pp. 4-6, Figure 1, Ministry of Finance data).
- Incidence: bottom 40% captured 29% of energy subsidies (World Bank 2014, cited in SRC-TG-162,
  p. 2); poorest quintile 14.9%, richest 25% of residential energy subsidy benefits (SRC-TG-162,
  Table 7, 2010 household survey basis).
- SOE deficit 4.4% of GDP in 2013 led by STEG; transfers to public enterprises about 2% to 7% of
  GDP, 2010-2013 (SRC-TG-163, p. 25).
- Automatic fuel formula commitment and electricity tariff path (SRC-TG-163, paras. 14, 23; MEFP
  para. 22).

## Why confidence is what it is

Medium. The history and the incidence rest on one World Bank working paper (tier B, not
peer-reviewed), which itself draws on Ministry of Finance data and IMF reports. The paper is
internally inconsistent on the 2013 energy figure (4.7% of GDP in Figure 1, "7% of the GDP" in one
sentence that seems to refer to all subsidies); this record uses 4.7% and notes it was a forecast.
The two incidence figures come from different methods and both point the same way (pro-rich in
absolute terms), but a 2019 ESCWA study reportedly finds the opposite and was not opened. The
claim that subsidies are "regressive" holds for absolute benefits per person; it was not checked
against benefits as a share of household spending, where the poorest quintile spends a similar
share on energy to the richest (SRC-TG-162).

## Links to other sectors

Not established with sources here. The same paper models food subsidies alongside energy
(SRC-TG-162), and diesel and electricity subsidies reach irrigation and agriculture, but no source
opened quantifies the agricultural share of energy subsidies. Listed as a lead, not a link.
