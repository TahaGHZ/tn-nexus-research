## Problem

Towns along the Tunisian coast grow mostly onto the farmland around them, which is often the most fertile and best-watered land. In Greater Tunis, 2,440 of the 3,970 ha urbanised between 1986 and 1991 were farmland (SRC-TG-845, citing Gafsi 1996). The government's 2016 Habitat III report says informal building has spread over fertile farmland. It also says public facilities and economic projects take about 400 ha of farmland a year through separate changes of vocation (SRC-TG-846). Local remote-sensing studies show the same pattern in Cap Bon and the Sahel. In Kelibia (Nabeul), built-up area on farmland went from 13 to 44 ha in 2012-2016 (SRC-TG-847). In Kalâa Kebira (Sousse), urban area grew from 890 to 1,307 ha in 2012-2023 while tree crops lost 621 ha (SRC-TG-848). The national scale is not known: no official series exists, and the figures that circulate disagree by an order of magnitude.

## Root causes

The symptom is farmland turned into housing, industry and infrastructure. The causes that the sources name:

- **Past housing and land policy (legacy decision).** A ministry-commissioned diagnosis says housing programmes since the 1970s caused the loss and fragmentation of thousands of hectares of fertile farmland, worst in Greater Tunis. It also says public land agencies pushed to develop land reserves bought cheaply in the 1970s (SRC-TG-845). Formal serviced land was scarce: the AFH serviced 1,094 ha in 1995-2014, about 55 ha a year for the whole country (SRC-TG-846). Households that could not get formal plots bought into informal subdivisions on farmland. The same report says the declassification procedure is too slow to get ahead of informal housing (SRC-TG-845).
- **Plot-by-plot conversion for public and economic projects.** The 2016 national report names changes of vocation for public facilities and economic projects (about 400 ha/yr) as one cause of sprawl. It says these projects encourage informal building around them (SRC-TG-846). The legal mechanism is described in FND-TG-841.
- **Coastal concentration.** The north-east and centre-east, 17.2% of the territory, hold about 61.6% of the population. The government links this to intense pressure on fertile farmland, alongside a weak national spatial-planning strategy and ineffective urban-planning tools (SRC-TG-846).
- **Weak local institutions.** The sources cite weak municipal technical means, planning documents (SDA, PAU) that are not binding or are outdated, and public land perimeters (PIF) created outside urban plans on farmland. One example is Ennasr II in Ariana, started in 1987 although the land was declassified only in 1993 (SRC-TG-845, SRC-TG-846).

## Evidence

**What is solid.** Official and peer-reviewed sources agree that urbanisation in coastal regions takes farmland (SRC-TG-845, SRC-TG-846, SRC-TG-847). In Greater Tunis, 61% of the land urbanised in 1986-1991 (2,440 of 3,970 ha) was farmland (SRC-TG-845, secondary citation of Gafsi 1996). For Gabès, the same report cites a thesis saying water competition and unplanned urbanisation together caused the loss of nearly 60% of the oasis's farmland, without a period (SRC-TG-845, citing Abdedaiem 2009).

**Rates disagree, and they measure different things:**
- About 400 ha/yr of farmland changed vocation for public facilities and economic projects (Habitat III national report, 2016, no stated period or method) (SRC-TG-846).
- 1,370 ha of farmland changed vocation in 2011-2021, about 137 ha/yr, from an ongoing DGAT study known only through press, geography unclear (SRC-TG-850). If it is national, it is far below the 400 ha/yr in SRC-TG-846. Both figures count only formal changes of vocation, not informal building.
- About 4,000 ha/yr of urban land consumption around 2025, nearly 40% of it unplanned. This is an expert's figure reported in the press, and it covers all land types, not only farmland (SRC-TG-852).
- "Over 20,000 ha/yr" (SRC-TG-850), "25,000 ha/yr" (SRC-TG-851) and "about 23,000 ha/yr estimated in the mid-1990s" (SRC-TG-850) are total farmland losses from erosion and urbanisation combined. None gives a source or method.

**The common belief does not hold as stated.** The often-quoted 20,000-25,000 ha/yr is not a measure of land lost to urbanisation. It combines erosion with urbanisation, traces back to a mid-1990s estimate with no stated method, and is ten to a hundred times larger than the formal change-of-vocation figures above.

**National aggregates do not show the loss.** FAO-based agricultural land (arable land, permanent crops and pasture) rose from 90,610 km2 in 1980 to 100,380 km2 in 2010, then eased to 96,469 km2 in 2023 (SRC-TG-849). Arable land fell from 3.19 Mha (1980) to 2.77 Mha (2023), but the series does not say why (SRC-TG-849). The problem is therefore one of quality and location: fertile, irrigated, near-market land is lost while marginal land is added elsewhere. It is not a measurable national shrinkage. A soil-loss framing does not fit this evidence, so the theme is land-structure.

**Local case studies (Cap Bon, Sahel):**
- Kelibia, Nabeul, 2012-2016: urban area 458 to 756 ha; built-up area on farmland 13 to 44 ha (Table 5). The text's "400%" does not match the table (SRC-TG-847).
- Kalâa Kebira, Sousse, 2012-2023: urban area 890 to 1,307 ha (+417 ha); tree crops -621 ha; mixed crops -111 ha; pasture and uncultivated land +315 ha. Urbanisation explains only part of the crop loss; drought and abandonment also play a role. This is a non-peer-reviewed preprint with internal errors (SRC-TG-848).
- Manouba, Ben Arous, Ariana: press reports cite AUGT studies giving the share of construction in protected zones as 66.5%, about 35% and over 50%. The base of these percentages is unclear (SRC-TG-850).

## Why confidence is what it is

Medium for the existence and direction of the problem: a government report (SRC-TG-846), a ministry-commissioned diagnosis (SRC-TG-845) and a peer-reviewed study (SRC-TG-847) agree, and the JORT shows conversions happening (FND-TG-841). Low for any rate. The national rates rest on a single undated government figure (400 ha/yr) or on press (1,370 ha, 4,000 ha/yr, 20,000-25,000 ha/yr). The Greater Tunis and Gabès figures are secondary citations, and the Kalâa Kebira study is a preprint with errors. Sfax and the Sahel beyond Kalâa Kebira are not quantified. Severity is set at medium because the national farmland base is not shrinking measurably. The loss is concentrated on high-value peri-urban and irrigated land, so local severity is likely higher.

## Links to other sectors

- **Water.** Safeguard zones are defined partly by irrigation and exploitable groundwater (SRC-TG-840), and prohibition zones include public irrigated perimeters. Building on these lands wastes public irrigation investment. The press reports a large share of Manouba construction in prohibition zones, i.e. public irrigated perimeters or forest (SRC-TG-850). In Gabès, urbanisation combined with competition for water is linked to the loss of nearly 60% of oasis farmland (SRC-TG-845). The Kelibia study links building in the hydraulic public domain to flood risk (SRC-TG-847), and the Habitat III report links sprawl to more frequent urban flooding (SRC-TG-846). Water-lane researchers may want to quantify irrigated perimeter area lost to building.
- **Energy.** None found beyond individual conversions for grid infrastructure (see FND-TG-841).
