## Problem

Tunisia's coastal towns grow mostly onto the farmland around them, which is often the most fertile and best-watered land. In Greater Tunis, 2,440 of the 3,970 ha urbanised between 1986 and 1991 were farmland (SRC-TG-845, citing Gafsi 1996). The government's 2016 Habitat III report says informal building has spread over fertile farmland. It also says public facilities and economic projects take about 400 ha of farmland a year through separate changes of vocation (SRC-TG-846). A 2018 CIHEAM chapter, citing the 2012 environmental profile of Tunisia, puts urban losses at 2,000-3,000 ha of farmland a year (SRC-TG-854).

Local remote-sensing studies show the same pattern in Cap Bon and the Sahel:
- Kelibia (Nabeul), 2012-2016: built-up area on farmland grew from 13 to 44 ha (SRC-TG-847).
- Kalâa Kebira (Sousse), 2012-2023: urban area grew from 890 to 1,307 ha while tree crops lost 621 ha (SRC-TG-848).

There is no official series. The figures that circulate range from 137 to 25,000 ha a year, because they measure different things.

## Root causes

The symptom is farmland turned into housing, industry and infrastructure. The causes named in the sources are below.

- **Past housing and land policy (legacy decision).** A ministry-commissioned diagnosis says housing programmes since the 1970s caused the loss and fragmentation of thousands of hectares of fertile farmland, worst in Greater Tunis. It also says public operators' sector logic, including the AFH's pressure to develop land reserves bought very cheaply in the 1970s, often overrode planning (SRC-TG-845). Formal serviced land stayed small: the AFH serviced 1,094 ha in 1995-2014, about 55 ha a year nationwide (SRC-TG-846). The same report names a weak housing policy for low-income groups as a cause of sprawl (SRC-TG-846, p. 20). It is our inference from those pages, not the report's own words, that small formal supply pushed households into informal subdivisions on farmland. The report itself credits the AFH's subdivisions with reducing unplanned sprawl onto farmland (SRC-TG-846, p. 21). The 2014 diagnosis adds that the declassification procedure is too slow to get ahead of informal housing (SRC-TG-845).
- **Siting public and economic projects on farmland case by case.** Public facilities and economic projects are placed on farmland through separate changes of vocation, which also encourages informal building around them (SRC-TG-846). The 400 ha/yr figure measures the result of this practice, not its cause. The legal mechanism is described in FND-TG-841.
- **Coastal concentration.** The north-east and centre-east, 17.2% of the territory, hold about 61.6% of the population. The government links this to intense pressure on fertile farmland, alongside a weak national spatial-planning strategy and ineffective planning tools (SRC-TG-846).
- **Weak local institutions and non-binding plans.** Municipalities have weak technical means (SRC-TG-846). The urbanisation zones in regional master plans (SDA) "ne revêtent pas de caractère obligatoire" (are not binding) and are disconnected from municipal needs (SRC-TG-845, p. 30). Public land perimeters (PIF) were created outside urban plans on farmland: Ennasr II in Ariana started in 1987, but the land was declassified only in 1993 (SRC-TG-845).

## Evidence

**What is solid.** Official, institutional and peer-reviewed sources agree that urbanisation in coastal regions takes farmland (SRC-TG-845, SRC-TG-846, SRC-TG-847, SRC-TG-854). Peri-urban land around Greater Tunis includes irrigated land, such as the lower Medjerda perimeter built in the 1950s (SRC-TG-854). For Greater Tunis in 1986-1991, 61% of the land urbanised (2,440 of 3,970 ha) was farmland (SRC-TG-845, secondary citation of Gafsi 1996). For Gabès, the same report cites a thesis: water competition plus unplanned urbanisation caused the loss of nearly 60% of the oasis's farmland, with no period given (SRC-TG-845, citing Abdedaiem 2009).

**Rates disagree, and they measure different things:**
- 2,000-3,000 ha/yr of farmland lost to urban pressure, around 2012, as a separate figure from 15,000-20,000 ha/yr lost to erosion. This is a secondary citation of the EU-funded 2012 environmental profile, which was not opened (SRC-TG-854).
- About 400 ha/yr of farmland changed vocation for public facilities and economic projects (Habitat III national report, 2016; no stated period or method) (SRC-TG-846).
- 1,370 ha changed vocation in 2011-2021, about 137 ha/yr, from an ongoing DGAT study known only through press, with unclear geography (SRC-TG-850). If national, it is well below the 400 ha/yr in SRC-TG-846.
- About 4,000 ha/yr of urban land consumption (no year stated; La Presse article of 18 May 2026), nearly 40% of it unplanned. This is an expert's figure reported in the press, and it covers all land types, not only farmland (SRC-TG-852).
- "Over 20,000 ha/yr" (SRC-TG-850), "25,000 ha/yr" (SRC-TG-851) and "about 23,000 ha/yr estimated in the mid-1990s" (SRC-TG-850) are total farmland losses from erosion and urbanisation combined. The Nawaat paragraph is footnoted to Elloumi (2011, CIHEAM), which could not be opened.

The change-of-vocation figures cover only formal decisions and miss informal building, which probably explains part of the gap with the 2,000-3,000 ha/yr estimate.

**The common belief does not hold as stated.** The often-quoted 20,000-25,000 ha/yr is not a measure of land lost to urbanisation. It combines erosion with urbanisation, and the one source that separates them attributes most of it to erosion (15,000-20,000 ha/yr) and only 2,000-3,000 ha/yr to urban pressure (SRC-TG-854). It is roughly 50 to 180 times the formal change-of-vocation figures above. That the later press figures descend from the mid-1990s estimate is our inference from the similar magnitudes and Nawaat's wording (SRC-TG-850).

**National aggregates do not show the loss.** FAO-based agricultural land (arable land, permanent crops and pasture) rose from 90,610 km2 in 1980 to 100,380 km2 in 2010, then eased to 96,469 km2 in 2023 (SRC-TG-849). Arable land fell from 3.19 Mha in 1980 to 2.77 Mha in 2023, but the series does not attribute the change to any cause (SRC-TG-849). The loss is concentrated on fertile, irrigated, near-market land. We hypothesise, without a source linking the two, that marginal land such as rangeland or new olive plantations is being added elsewhere and masks it in the totals. A soil-loss framing does not fit this evidence, so the theme is land-structure.

**Local case studies (Cap Bon, Sahel):**
- **Kelibia, Nabeul, 2012-2016:** urban area grew from 458 to 756 ha; built-up area on farmland from 13 to 44 ha (Table 7 for 458 to 756 ha; Table 5 for 13 to 44 ha). The "400%" stated in the paper's text does not match its table (SRC-TG-847).
- **Kalâa Kebira, Sousse, 2012-2023:** urban area +417 ha (890 to 1,307 ha); tree crops -621 ha; mixed crops -111 ha; pasture and uncultivated land +315 ha. Urbanisation explains only part of the crop loss; drought and abandonment also play a role. The source is an unreviewed preprint with internal errors (SRC-TG-848).
- **Manouba, Ben Arous, Ariana:** the press cites AUGT studies giving the share of construction in protected zones as 66.5%, about 35% and over 50%. The base of these percentages is unclear (SRC-TG-850).

## Why confidence is what it is

Confidence that the problem exists and in which direction it runs is medium. A government report (SRC-TG-846), a ministry-commissioned diagnosis (SRC-TG-845), a CIHEAM chapter (SRC-TG-854) and a peer-reviewed study (SRC-TG-847) agree, and the official gazette shows conversions happening (FND-TG-841).

Confidence in any rate is low:
- The only urbanisation-specific national estimate (2,000-3,000 ha/yr) is a secondary citation of a 2012 consultancy report that was not opened.
- The 400 ha/yr is one undated government figure.
- The other rates come from the press.
- The Greater Tunis and Gabès figures are secondary citations, and the Kalâa Kebira study is a preprint with errors.
- Sfax and the Sahel beyond Kalâa Kebira are not quantified.

Severity is set at medium because the national farmland base is not shrinking measurably. The loss falls on high-value peri-urban and irrigated land, so local severity is likely higher.

## Links to other sectors

**Water.** Peri-urban land lost around Greater Tunis includes irrigated land, such as the lower Medjerda perimeter (SRC-TG-854). Safeguard zones are defined partly by irrigation and exploitable groundwater, and prohibition zones include public irrigated perimeters (SRC-TG-840). Building on these lands wastes public irrigation investment. Further links:
- The press reports a large share of Manouba construction in prohibition zones, i.e. public irrigated perimeters or forest (SRC-TG-850).
- In Gabès, urbanisation combined with competition for water is linked to the loss of nearly 60% of oasis farmland (SRC-TG-845).
- The Kelibia study links building in the hydraulic public domain to flooding vulnerability (SRC-TG-847), and the Habitat III report links sprawl to more frequent urban flooding (SRC-TG-846).

Water-lane researchers may want to quantify the irrigated perimeter area lost to building.

**Energy.** None found beyond individual conversions for grid infrastructure (see FND-TG-841).
