## Problem

A large part of Tunisian farmland does not carry a title that a bank can take as security. Citing the World Bank (2006), the FAO/CIHEAM study states that only half of the land potential is registered, nearly a third of farms have no title or possession certificate, and about 60% of titles are "frozen", meaning the legal record no longer matches who actually holds the land after inheritance, sales or gifts (SRC-TG-781). Collective land, of which half of some 3 million ha in the centre and south had been settled into private plots (SRC-TG-781), was made unseizable and inalienable by law 64-28 as enacted in 1964, mortgageable only to official agricultural lenders with ministerial authorisation (SRC-TG-783); how the 2016 amendment changed this was not checked. Both FAO/CIHEAM and FTDES say most small farmers lack titles and are therefore shut out of bank credit (SRC-TG-781, SRC-TG-782). In 2003-04, 87.5% of farm heads did not apply for any credit (SRC-TG-780). In July 2026 a draft decree-law was reported to aim at converting southern collective land into individual, transferable and bankable titles through a Land Court with special powers (SRC-TG-792). This suggests the problem is still open.

## Root causes

The symptom is land that cannot be sold, leased formally or mortgaged. The causes the sources give are:

- **Legal status of collective land (legacy decision, 1964).** Law 64-28 of 4 June 1964 declared collective land unseizable, inalienable and imprescriptible, belonging to the user group under state supervision. It bars sale and mgharsa plantation contracts without the agriculture minister's special authorisation, and allows mortgages only to official agricultural credit or land-loan bodies, for permanent improvements, with joint ministerial approval (SRC-TG-783). The law also provided for these lands to become development cooperatives (SRC-TG-783), a link to the 1960s cooperative experiment described in FND-TG-782. Later amendments, the latest being law 2016-69, were not opened (see `data_gaps`).
- **Slow, fragmented registration.** Registration and settlement involve at least three ministries: State Domains (register), Equipment (survey and mapping) and Justice (the Land Court) (SRC-TG-781).
- **Titles not updated after inheritance.** Inheritance is the main way owner-operated parcels are acquired (SRC-TG-780). When heirs do not record the division, titles "freeze" (SRC-TG-781). This connects the title problem to the holding fragmentation in FND-TG-780.
- **Bank collateral requirements.** Lenders require real collateral and profitability; FTDES names the land title as the collateral most small farmers cannot provide (SRC-TG-781, SRC-TG-782).

## Evidence

- Registration: 50% of land registered; nearly one-third of farms without title or possession certificate; about 60% of titles frozen; settlement handled by three ministries (SRC-TG-781, citing World Bank 2006 and Gharbi 1998).
- Collective land: half of about 3 million ha in the centre and south settled into private property; habous land fell from 1 million ha after independence to about 80,000 ha (SRC-TG-781).
- Legal regime: articles 1 and 5 of law 64-28 (SRC-TG-783).
- Credit, 2003-04: 87.5% of farm heads did not apply; 6.2% applied and were refused. Non-applicants cited access difficulty (28%) and debt risk (27%); 2% said they were already indebted and 1.8% cited high interest (SRC-TG-780). Help from relatives was a more frequent source of investment finance than bank credit (SRC-TG-780).
- Qualitative: "a large majority" of farmers have no title or only uncertain titles (SRC-TG-781); "most" farmers lack a title (SRC-TG-782).
- Current policy signal: draft decree-law (July 2026) to convert southern collective land reserves into individual, transferable and bankable titles (SRC-TG-792).
- Investment law: law 2016-71 frees investors to acquire, lease or operate non-agricultural real estate only, so agricultural land stays outside this liberalisation (SRC-TG-794).

## Why confidence is what it is

Confidence is low:

- The quantitative title figures are second-hand. FAO/CIHEAM cites them from a 2006 World Bank study we could not find and from a 1998 book (SRC-TG-781). They describe the situation roughly 20-28 years ago.
- The claim that missing titles cause credit exclusion is an assertion in two tier B reports (SRC-TG-781, SRC-TG-782). It is not tested against data. The only national credit data (SRC-TG-780) show very low demand for credit and cite access difficulty and debt risk. They do not break results down by title status, so they are equally consistent with low income and climate risk being the main barrier, as FAO/CIHEAM itself also says (SRC-TG-781).
- The legal text of law 64-28 is a tier A primary source (SRC-TG-783). It is the original 1964 version, read from a poor scan, and amendments up to 2016 were not read.
- The 2026 reform signal rests on tier C press (SRC-TG-792).

The legal constraint is well established. How much land is affected today, and how far it, rather than poverty and risk, drives credit exclusion, are not established.

## Links to other sectors

Water: FND-TG-741 records that Kebili's unauthorised palm extensions, now most of the region's date area, are planted on collective land outside official oases and irrigated from illicit boreholes. Collective tenure in the south is therefore tied to informal groundwater use. This run did not add sources on that link. Energy: none found.
