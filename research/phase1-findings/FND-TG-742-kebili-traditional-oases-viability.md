## Problem

The old collective oases of Kebili are three-layer systems of palms, fruit trees and crops, and they are under a combined squeeze. Their area is small and nearly static. Legal oases (which may also include the few state-created ones) covered at most 10,485 ha in 2018, against an estimated 32,700 ha of extensions in 2020 (SRC-TG-740). Dagher et al. round this to 10,500 ha of "traditional oases" (SRC-TG-741).

- **Water:** water turns in traditional oases come from collective boreholes managed by water user associations and range from 25 to 60 days apart (SRC-TG-741).
- **Crop layers:** the typical three layers and high crop diversity are now found less often than expected in traditional oases (SRC-TG-745).
- **Labour:** real labour costs for date production grew 14.5% a year from 2015 to 2022 (SRC-TG-741).
- **2030 projection:** a business-as-usual scenario covers plots in traditional oases without access to an individual borehole. In the survey, 19% of farmers who owned plots in traditional oases had their own borehole. On the no-individual-borehole plots, labour would reach 86% of Deglet Nour production costs and Deglet Nour income would turn negative, while common varieties would remain profitable (SRC-TG-741). The paper does not establish that the farmer-level 19% share exactly equals the share of plots in the scenario.
- **Abandonment:** the authors report a growing number of abandoned plots in 2023 and expect it to raise pest and disease pressure (SRC-TG-741). A policy brief says many family farmers neglect or sometimes abandon their old-oasis land to concentrate on extensions (SRC-TG-747).

## Root causes

Falling income and abandonment are the symptoms. The documented contributing conditions are:

- **Aquifer drawdown and deteriorating collective networks.** Artesian wells had all dried out by the early 2000s, and pumped flows in collective networks were decreasing (SRC-TG-740). Administrators describe inefficient management and poor maintenance of those networks (SRC-TG-740). Dagher et al. expect water in traditional oases to get scarcer because water user associations are increasingly hard to run (SRC-TG-741). Interviewed CRDA staff attributed additional reduced water availability and drainage problems to nearby extensions (SRC-TG-740, section 3.3.2, actors' discourse); this is interview evidence, not a measured causal result. Some farmers in traditional oases drill individual boreholes to supplement water supplied by the associations (SRC-TG-741). The aquifer drawdown itself belongs to the water lane.
- **Labour.** Young people are less interested in farming and migrate. Labour costs have risen faster than inflation, and Deglet Nour harvesting and tending need a lot of labour (SRC-TG-741).
- **Land fragmentation.** Traditional-oasis plots are typically inherited, 1 ha or less; repeated partitioning splits plots into small holdings (SRC-TG-745). Old-oasis plots are "très petites et morcelées" (very small and fragmented) (SRC-TG-747).
- **Soil.** Mekki et al. report poor soil fertility and biodiversity loss in traditional oases; the inspected passage does not establish that biodiversity loss caused the fertility decline (SRC-TG-740). A field survey reports fertility loss exacerbated by saline irrigation water (SRC-TG-745). Interviewed CRDA staff also said that deep pumping raised shallow water tables and caused drainage problems (SRC-TG-740, section 3.3.2, actors' discourse).
- **Link to the variety choice.** In the 2021-2022 survey years, under mite and water stress, common varieties were more profitable than Deglet Nour in traditional oases. Farmers seldom replant there because doing so requires removing existing palms (SRC-TG-741). See FND-TG-740.

## Evidence

- Area: traditional oases cover 18,590 ha nationally (2018 survey) (SRC-TG-743). ONAGRI gives this as 32.4% of oasis area, but it divides the 2018 area by the 2022 total; on the 2018 survey total it would be about 32.8% (SRC-TG-743). In Kebili, legal oases covered at most 10,485 ha in 2018 (SRC-TG-740). The source does not say whether this includes state-created oases, so the traditional area may be somewhat smaller.
- Water turns: 25-60 days between turns in traditional oases (SRC-TG-741).
- Agro-biodiversity: in a 52-plot survey (2020), high crop diversity and three vertical layers were less frequent than expected in traditional oases. Water availability and irrigation frequency were the key factors behind the change (SRC-TG-745).
- Projection: by 2030, under continued labour-cost growth, labour would be 84% of Deglet Nour production costs in extensions and 86% in traditional oases. Deglet Nour income on plots in traditional oases without an individual borehole would become negative (SRC-TG-741). The survey found that 19% of traditional-oasis farmers had their own borehole (SRC-TG-741); that farmer share is not necessarily the same as the plot share.

## Why confidence is what it is

Low.

- **The projection is narrow.** It comes from a single peer-reviewed study (SRC-TG-741) of 123 farmers in two parts of Kebili. It is a simple extrapolation of one cost trend, with no water, climate or price modelling. It applies only to traditional-oasis plots without an individual borehole. The 2030 result depends heavily on labour costs continuing to grow at 14.5% a year in real terms.
- **Some cause-and-effect claims rest on interviews.** The effect of extensions on the old oases' water is what CRDA staff said in interviews, not a measured result (SRC-TG-740).
- **Other evidence is thin.** The biodiversity evidence comes from a small sample (SRC-TG-745). Abandonment is reported only qualitatively.
- **Kebili only.** The sources opened do not cover the traditional oases of Gabes, Tozeur or Gafsa.

## Links to other sectors

- **Water:** two sources point to falling collective water supply: dried-up artesian wells and decaying networks (SRC-TG-740, SRC-TG-741). According to CRDA staff interviews, pumping by extensions reduces old-oasis water availability and raises drainage problems (SRC-TG-740); these are attributed interview accounts. Saline irrigation water is a water-quality issue for the water lane (SRC-TG-745).

## Leads for phase 2

- Promotion of common date varieties (Kenta, Alig, Gosbi) in traditional oases as a lower-labour, lower-risk option (SRC-TG-741)
- PDES "programme d'économie d'eau dans les oasis" (Japanese-funded concrete seguias from 1985), whose networks are now reported as leaking (SRC-TG-742)
- Nefzaoua and APIOS projects: integration of adjoining extensions into collective water turns, late 1980s (SRC-TG-740)
- Water user associations (GDA) managing collective boreholes (SRC-TG-741)

## Verification

Reopened the original study of individual farmers and plots (SRC-TG-741). Collective boreholes are managed by water-user associations; a farmer-level borehole measure is distinct from the share of plots. The 2021–22 field evidence reflects mite and water stress. The 2030 result is a study projection by plot type and variety, not an observed loss for the whole governorate.
