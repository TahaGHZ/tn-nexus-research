## Problem

The oases of Kébili (Nefzaoua) and Tozeur (Djerid) were once watered by artesian springs fed from the Saharan deep aquifers. Deep boreholes replaced them and expanded the irrigated area onto non-renewable water; SRC-TG-630 attributes the overdraft to supply-led development and, from 2011, to unauthorised private wells. Withdrawals from the shared Saharan aquifer system (SASS) rose from 0.6 billion m3/yr in the early 1970s to 2.5 billion in 2000, against recharge of about 1 billion m3/yr. The system crossed into overexploitation in the early 1980s (SRC-TG-629, SRC-TG-628). Tunisia's share in 2000 was 0.55 billion m3/yr (SRC-TG-628). The southern oasis area went from 17,500 ha in 1992 to about 56,600 ha in 2017-2018 (SRC-TG-630, SRC-TG-634). In the 2019 water-balance study's undated table of mobilised resources, the remaining springs yield 2.85 Mm3, against 791 Mm3 exploited, mostly from boreholes (SRC-TG-630). At Rjim Maâtoug, described as the last flowing confined aquifer, the CT head fell about 35 m between 1977 and 2016 (SRC-TG-630). This finding records the legacy decisions associated with the overdraft described in FND-TG-625.

## Root causes

1. **Dates as an export earner (from 1956).** After independence, policy in the region aimed to finance development and earn exports to balance trade. For agriculture it emphasised date-palm growing in the Djerid and Nefzaoua, and modern oases were developed in Tozeur and Kébili from the 1970s (SRC-TG-630). The colonial period had already started this, with artesian boreholes and Deglet Nour monoculture for export, and the first drying of springs (Vernin 2020 and MEDD 2015, as cited in SRC-TG-634).
2. **The Southern Water Master Plan and state oasis creation (1980s).** Rjim Maâtoug began in 1984 as a 300 ha army-led pilot under the Plan Directeur des Eaux du Sud, 1980-1992. An office under the Ministry of National Defence was created in 1989 (SRC-TG-634). Its stated aims were a green belt against the desert, settling nomads and adding 20,000 t/yr of Deglet Nour. It was given 2,000 l/s of groundwater for 2,500 ha. The first tranche (1990-2001, 1,152 ha, 17 boreholes) cost 32 MDT, financed by Tunisia (10), the EU (15) and Italy (7). A second tranche (2002-2012, 12 boreholes) was funded with Italy, and a third ran 2013-2023 (SRC-TG-634). Border settlement was an explicit concern (Daoued 2011, as cited in SRC-TG-634). All state extensions were Deglet Nour only; even the 15% of other varieties required by the planting protocol was not respected (SRC-TG-634). The programme is presented as a success and was extended with a new 300 ha tranche at El Mohdeth in July 2020, again with Italian funding (SRC-TG-634). The past decision is therefore still being repeated. Its boreholes are a small share of the overdraft (about 2,500 ha of about 56,600 ha, SRC-TG-630, SRC-TG-634); it is documented here as a decision, not shown to be the cause of the spring decline.
3. **Liberalisation.** The 1986 structural adjustment plan (PAS) and 1987 agricultural adjustment plan (PASA) privatised access to water and land. Irrigation water in the oases became a paid service (SRC-TG-634). Date-market liberalisation raised Deglet Nour prices, which farmers and the CRDA cite as a driver of extensions (SRC-TG-631). State disengagement and export prices in the 2000s are also cited by the 2019 water-balance study (SRC-TG-630). The state also created collective-land management councils that allocated land and triggered one type of extension (SRC-TG-631).
4. **A rule declared but not applied.** In 1985 the government created safeguard perimeters for the CI and CT in the Nefzaoua (Decrees 85-1104 and 85-1109) (SRC-TG-632). The extensions that followed are illicit because they draw on boreholes inside these perimeters (SRC-TG-632), but enforcement was brief and weak, and in practice amounted to laisser-faire (SRC-TG-632). In 1991 regional officials already refused to map the extensions because they eased social tension (Bisson 1991, as cited in SRC-TG-631). Illicit extensions in Kébili were 4,085 ha in 1995 (Hajji 1996, as cited in SRC-TG-634).

The symptom is the severe reduction of natural spring flows and the loss of artesian pressure. The sources describe overdraft from supply-led development and unauthorised wells; they do not date the spring loss or separate the effect of state oases, which are about 2,500 ha of about 56,600 ha. This mirrors the national supply-side strategy in FND-TG-426 and the drilling incentives in FND-TG-401.

## Evidence

- **SASS-wide trend.** 0.6 billion m3/yr (1970) to 2.5 billion m3/yr (2000), with 1,200 of the 8,800 water points in Tunisia (SRC-TG-628). Then 2.7 billion m3/yr (2012), overexploitation "dont le seuil a été franchi au début des années 1980" (SRC-TG-629). The overexploitation threshold was crossed in the same decade as the Southern Water Master Plan; the sources do not attribute it to Tunisia alone, since Algeria draws most of the SASS (1.5 of 2.5 billion m3 in 2000) (SRC-TG-628).
- **Springs.** The 2019 water-balance study describes the supply-led strategy reaching its limits as causing a "tarissement total" of the springs that were the oases' main irrigation source (SRC-TG-630, p. 18). Its own table of mobilised resources, which carries no year, still lists 17 springs giving 2.85 Mm3, so aggregate evidence supports a severe reduction, not total disappearance. OSS lists spring and foggara drying, reduced artesian pressure and deeper boreholes as consequences across the basin (SRC-TG-629).
- **Artesian pressure.** In the same undated table, Kébili has 714 artesian points yielding 79 Mm3, against 2,848 pumped points yielding 327 Mm3 (SRC-TG-630, Tableau 3, p. 30). Rjim Maâtoug's CT head fell about 35 m over 1977-2016, accelerating after 1995 with rising exploitation in Kébili and Tozeur. The Djerid CT fell about 1 m/yr over 2000-2014. Downstream declines of 1-1.5 m/yr are making artesian flow disappear (SRC-TG-630).
- **Area.** Southern oases grew from 17,500 ha (1992) to 56,589 ha (2017) (SRC-TG-630). The national oasis survey gives 56,654 ha in 2018, doubled over 2007-2017; 37,387 ha are "new private oases" (SRC-TG-634).
- **Exports.** Over 2008-2018, date export volume rose 1.7-fold and export value 3.6-fold, with importing markets rising from 57 to 85 (SRC-TG-634).
- **Cost of the legacy.** The ministry's 2017 report lists a programme to replace 55 very deep oasis boreholes (over 850 m) irrigating 20,000 ha, costed at 150 MDT with EBRD financing (RNE 2017, PDF p. 196; document in SRC-TG-402, extract not yet in that record).

## Why confidence is what it is

Medium. The trend data (SASS withdrawals, oasis areas, Rjim Maâtoug piezometry) come from OSS reports (tier B) and DGRE tables reproduced in a World Bank-filed study. The policy history rests on three tier B reports and one peer-reviewed article, and several key facts are second-hand: the PDES dates, Rjim Maâtoug figures (Defence Ministry website via an FAO-commissioned report), the 1985 decrees (via a research policy note) and official tolerance (Bisson 1991, via a peer-reviewed article). Primary texts (PDES, JORT decrees) were not opened. The causal weight of each decision cannot be separated with the data found: state oases (2,500 ha at Rjim Maâtoug) are small next to private extensions (32,700 ha). Their role is as a model and precedent, and as the source of the public boreholes that extensions later tapped (SRC-TG-631), more than as the main consumer. The year the springs stopped flowing is not dated.



## Links to other sectors

- **Agriculture (dates-oases):** the policy created a Deglet Nour monoculture, which the FAO-commissioned report calls the "talon d'Achille" (Achilles heel) of the Rjim Maâtoug programme (SRC-TG-634). The move from free spring water to paid pumped water raised farmers' costs in the old oases (SRC-TG-634); many family farmers now give less weight to their old-oasis plots, sometimes abandoning them, and concentrate on the extensions (SRC-TG-632).
- **Energy:** replacing springs with boreholes made oasis water dependent on pumping energy; deeper boreholes raise pumping costs (SRC-TG-629, SRC-TG-630). The CI water is strongly artesian and geothermal (about 70 °C) (SRC-TG-630).
