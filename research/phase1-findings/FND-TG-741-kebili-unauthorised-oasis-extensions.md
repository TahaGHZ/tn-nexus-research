## Problem

Kebili's palm groves are now mostly unauthorised private "extensions" planted on collective land outside the official oases and irrigated from illicit boreholes. Ministry surveys counted about 5,240 ha of oases in Kebili in 1976 and about 7,000 ha of extensions by 1996. A research estimate puts extensions at 32,700 ha in 2020. Over the same period legal oases grew only from about 8,315 ha (1996) to at most 10,485 ha (2018) (SRC-TG-740). In 2020 the extensions produced about 85% of Kebili's dates (SRC-TG-740). A 2024 brief by the same team says about 80% (SRC-TG-747). The authors call them the largest illicit groundwater-based farming area in Tunisia (SRC-TG-740). They are illicit because their boreholes sit inside the groundwater safeguard perimeters created by decrees 85-1104 and 85-1109 of 1985, which cover almost all of Kebili (SRC-TG-747). Nationally, private new oases covered 37,387 ha in the 2018 survey (65.1% of oasis area), against 18,590 ha of traditional oases and 677 ha of organised state oases (SRC-TG-743). The FAO report says Kebili's oasis area grew fivefold, from 7,600 ha in 1992 to 36,560 ha in 2018 (SRC-TG-742). The 1992 figure probably leaves out extensions, which were not counted until the late 1990s (SRC-TG-740). Counting them, Mekki et al.'s figures give about 15,300 ha in 1996 (8,315 ha legal plus 7,000 ha extensions) (SRC-TG-740), so growth is about 2.8 times (2018 legal area plus 2020 extension estimate, about 43,200 ha, against 1996). The two end-points mix years, so this is approximate. A CRDA Kebili official quoted by the press in 2023 gives a similar split: about 40,000 ha in total, 10,500 ha of public irrigated areas and nearly 30,000 ha of private holdings around more than 8,000 unauthorised wells (SRC-TG-748; tier C).

## Root causes

The symptom is uncontrolled growth of palm area. The contributing factors include how the state handled demand for land and water:

- **Ban with short-lived, ineffective enforcement and few alternatives (legacy).** In 1985 the administration defined a drilling-ban zone covering almost all of Kebili to protect the barely renewable aquifers (SRC-TG-747). The government, however, had "no clear plans" for palm grove areas (SRC-TG-740). Interviewed administrators said that apart from about 500 ha of new oases created by the Plan Directeur des Eaux du Sud (PDES), the administration created few oases (SRC-TG-740). The FAO report, however, places Rjim Maatoug (2,500 ha, started 1984) within the PDES (SRC-TG-742). **The sources conflict on PDES creation totals: interviewed administrators cite about 500 ha, while the FAO report includes 2,500 ha at Rjim Maatoug (SRC-TG-740, SRC-TG-742).** In the late 1980s the state folded some extensions next to old oases into collective water turns. By the mid-1990s a continuing decline in surveillance and sanctions was reported (SRC-TG-740). The administration tried coercive measures such as rig seizures and prosecutions, but they were short-lived and ineffective (SRC-TG-747). A CRDA manager regretted not creating public irrigated oases on collective land: "we would have avoided all these illicit drillings" (SRC-TG-740).
- **Cheaper access to water and energy.** Low-cost drilling arrived in the mid-1980s. Owners of subsidised Deglet Nour cold stores passed their legal grid connection on to boreholes in extensions. Solar pumps spread from 2014, and 2,358 solar panels were detected in Kebili (SRC-TG-740).
- **Push from the old oases and pull from prices.** Traditional oases became less profitable because of land fragmentation, falling fertility and water deficits; artesian wells had all dried out by the early 2000s. Meanwhile market liberalisation raised Deglet Nour prices (SRC-TG-740).
- **2011.** After the revolution, thousands of boreholes were drilled in 2011 and the CRDA could not intervene, according to the study's account of the period (SRC-TG-740). In places it even approved electricity connections it suspected would serve new groves (SRC-TG-740). The FAO report puts post-revolution illicit wells in Kebili at 9,000-10,000 (SRC-TG-742).

## Evidence

- **Mid-1990s extension area, two values:** 4,085 ha of illicit extensions in 1995 (Hajji, relayed by SRC-TG-742) and about 7,000 ha from the 1994-96 Ministry survey (Kassah, relayed by SRC-TG-740). The sources disagree; both are recorded.
- Area growth: 540 ha/year (1996-2010), 2,073 ha/year (2010-2016) and 1,434 ha/year (2016-2020) (SRC-TG-740).
- Illicit boreholes: several dozen in the 1970s, 1,500 in the 1990s, 3,500 in 2010 and 9,678 in 2017 (SRC-TG-740, relaying CRDA 2018).
- **Area figures for Kebili disagree.** Legal 10,485 ha plus extension estimate 32,700 ha gives about 43,200 ha (SRC-TG-740). The 2018 oasis survey gives 36,560 ha (SRC-TG-742), and DGPA gives 38,000 ha for 2022 (SRC-TG-743). The study says official surveys omit some unauthorised land (SRC-TG-740), a possible explanation for the gap. Dagher et al. repeat the 32,700 ha (SRC-TG-741), so that figure has one origin, not two.
- Mekki et al. also state that extensions give 80.7% of national date production (SRC-TG-740). This cannot match 85% of Kebili's output when Kebili is about 73% of national output (SRC-TG-742), so the figure is not used.
- Effects on the old oases: interviewed CRDA staff attributed reduced water availability in the old oases from the 2000s to nearby extensions and said deep pumping raised shallow water tables and caused drainage problems (SRC-TG-740, section 3.3.2, actors' discourse). These are interviewees' explanations, not independently measured causal findings. In extensions, the study reports farmer-built drainage networks whose effectiveness was uncertain and no negotiated approach to hydromorphy and soil salinisation (SRC-TG-740). See FND-TG-742.
- Policy response: about 3,750 ha of mutakhma extensions (those adjoining old oases) were legalised after 2017 and given subsidies for drip irrigation, basins and sand amendment. These subsidies were later put on hold for lack of a legal basis. A 2018 CRDA plan to subsidise all other extensions was also put on hold (SRC-TG-740). In July 2021, with the AFA, the state tried to legalise extensions by creating irrigated public areas, starting with the mutakhma (SRC-TG-740). Other extensions still have no access to subsidies (SRC-TG-740). In the Douz districts the disconnected extensions cover 7,991 ha (76.9% of extension area there), far more than the mutakhma at 751 ha (SRC-TG-740).

## Why confidence is what it is

Medium. The trajectory rests on one peer-reviewed study (SRC-TG-740), plus a later policy brief by the same team (SRC-TG-747) that is not independent of it, that combines Ministry and CRDA surveys with remote sensing and interviews. The FAO report independently shows strong growth from Ministry survey data: 7,600 ha in 1992, 15,960 ha in 2001 and 36,560 ha in 2018 (SRC-TG-742). The 2001 figure is consistent with the mid-1990s reconstruction. A CRDA official quoted in the press in 2023 gives a broadly similar reported public/private split (SRC-TG-748). That is tier C and comes from the same office Mekki et al. draw on, so it corroborates rather than independently confirms. The weak points:

- The 2020 extension area is a research estimate.
- Official and research area totals disagree.
- Borehole counts are CRDA figures relayed second-hand.
- The post-revolution weakening of CRDA control and the 2011 drilling surge are reported from interviews in one study; independent CRDA records were not opened.

The water-resource consequences belong to the water lane and are only summarised here.


## Links to other sectors

- **Water:** the extensions run on illicit pumping from the deep aquifers. Mekki et al. report a 24.7 m fall in piezometric level at Smida between 1991 and 2020, and rising salinity in the shallow water table, above 5 g/l in one extension (SRC-TG-740). The aquifer balance belongs to the water lane.
- **Energy:** pumping runs on diesel, informal grid connections (including through subsidised cold stores) and solar. Farmers in Blidette say solar pumps are rarely switched off, so solar has increased water use (SRC-TG-740).

## Leads for phase 2

- 2017 legalisation of mutakhma extensions with subsidies for drip irrigation, basins and sand amendment, later suspended (SRC-TG-740)
- July 2021 initiative with the Agence Foncière Agricole (AFA) to create irrigated public areas from extensions (SRC-TG-740)
- Discussions between the Ministry of Agriculture, APIA and AFA on legal status for all extensions (SRC-TG-740)
- Proposed bargain in SRC-TG-740: legalisation in exchange for water-use efficiency and a cap on planted area
- Massire project multi-actor workshop in Kebili (2022): a "win-win contract" idea that includes metering solar-pumped water or selling surplus solar power to STEG (SRC-TG-747)
- GDA Bsissi (Gabes) collective groundwater management, visited as a model (SRC-TG-747)
- Groundwater safeguard decrees 85-1104 and 85-1109 (1985) (SRC-TG-747)
- Rjim Maatoug and the Mohdeth extension (300 ha, inaugurated 2020) as state oasis-creation models (SRC-TG-742)

## Verification

The Kébili studies support the extent of private extension and report mechanisms described by farmers and administrators. Interview accounts are identified as such. Administrative approval dates, the timing of enforcement changes and their causal effects are unconfirmed (SRC-TG-740, SRC-TG-747).
