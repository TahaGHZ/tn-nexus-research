## Problem

Tunisia's official energy accounts omit quantities of fuel supplied through the parallel market, leaving a material measurement gap in transport-fuel consumption. ONEM's retrospective national balance for 2010-2019 says ordinary diesel was the road fuel most affected by smuggling and describes possible effects of the parallel market on the apparent changes in transport demand. It also cautions that attempts to estimate the additional volume produced highly divergent results, not comparable with extrapolating the pre-2011 trend, and that the additional contribution cannot be determined reliably from that approach (SRC-TG-130). This supports a historical measurement problem; it does not provide a quantified national smuggling series.

A 2013 World Bank working paper separately states that roughly 25% of Tunisian fuel consumption was informal imports from Algeria (SRC-TG-310). The paper's footnote compares STIR fuel imports with national consumption but does not show how that comparison isolates informal imports; the printed mass units are also evidently erroneous. The 25% is therefore an attributed historical estimate, not a direct series or a verified measurement. The same paper gives a separate estimate of 921,600 m³/year from a conditional van-activity extrapolation. Its arithmetic follows its stated assumptions (3,000 vans × 60% carrying fuel × 1.6 m³ per load × 320 days), but those assumptions are not a measured national count and the paper does not clearly date the estimate to calendar 2013. Keep the two estimates distinct and do not use either as a current share or volume.

The World Bank paper reports that fuel-price and tax differences, including a substantially lower price in Algeria, created incentives for informal trade at the time. ONEM's later official series contextualizes why the amount remains uncertain, but it does not independently validate the World Bank estimates.

## Root causes

The paper attributes the incentive to cross-border price differences associated with subsidy and tax regimes. This is a source-reported economic explanation. It does not establish that this factor alone caused observed changes in official transport demand, nor does it quantify the resulting informal volume. ONEM identifies multiple possible influences on road-fuel demand and on any estimate of parallel-market supply, including vehicle-fleet growth, fuel-price changes, purchasing power and energy-efficiency measures (SRC-TG-130).

## Evidence

- **Official series and interpretation (tier A):** ONEM's 2010-2019 retrospective distinguishes official road-transport consumption by product from additional fuel supplied through smuggling. It says ordinary diesel was most affected; estimates of its additional parallel-market contribution were highly divergent and not comparable with extrapolation of the pre-2011 trend. It concludes a reliable additional volume is difficult to determine and notes that adding the quantities to a national balance requires corresponding import and source-country export accounting to avoid imbalance or double counting (SRC-TG-130). The headline that official accounts omit parallel-market fuel rests on this: ONEM says integrating those quantities as final consumption would require counterpart accounts, which implies they are not currently in the balance, but ONEM does not state the omission in a single sentence.
- **Sourced reporting, not direct measurement (tier B):** The 2013 World Bank working paper attributes a roughly 25% national fuel-consumption share to informal Algerian imports. Its footnote gives STIR fuel imports as "2,790 million tons" and national fuel consumption as "3,746 million tons", using a stated estimated density of 0.792 t/m³. Those printed mass magnitudes are not credible as written, and the footnote does not transparently derive an informal-import share from the two quantities (SRC-TG-310).
- **Separate estimate, not an observation (tier B):** The paper's 921,600 m³/year estimate is explicitly conditional on assumed van activity and load. The multiplication is arithmetically correct, but the result inherits the assumptions and lacks a clearly stated observation year (SRC-TG-310).

## Why confidence is what it is

Low. The central finding is a measurement gap documented by the official ONEM retrospective, not a confirmed size of smuggling. ONEM is tier A for the official energy balance and its own account of estimation limitations. The numeric smuggling claims come from a 2013 World Bank working paper (tier B) and are estimates rather than direct observations; one method is incompletely explained, while the other depends on assumed van activity. The sources do not provide a comparable current estimate.

## Links to other sectors

Informal fuel trade links border commerce, transport-fuel supply and public revenue. The reviewed evidence describes these links qualitatively; this finding does not quantify their current economic effects.

## Verification

The World Bank working paper is dated and documents its estimation assumptions. It supports a historical finding on informal fuel trade, not a present-day import share.
