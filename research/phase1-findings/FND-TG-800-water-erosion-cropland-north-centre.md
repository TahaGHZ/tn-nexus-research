## Problem

Water erosion is the main soil problem of Tunisia's rainfed cereal belt in the Tell, the Dorsale and the central uplands. The figures the government uses are 3.56 million ha affected, 71% of it strongly to very strongly eroded, and a loss of the equivalent of 11,000 ha of productive land every year (SRC-TG-800). These are older figures (2007 and 2014) quoted again in the 2020 national desertification programme. Measured sediment yields from small catchments of the Dorsale and centre ran from 1.4 to 24 t/ha/yr in 1993-1999. One hill reservoir in six was completely silted after ten years (SRC-TG-802). The national programme reports that about a quarter of the utilised agricultural area has received soil and water conservation (CES) works (SRC-TG-800). It also says control of erosion is still below expectations, citing insufficient credits and weak planning (SRC-TG-800).

## Root causes

The symptoms are silted reservoirs, gullies and thinning soils. The causes are how the land is farmed and how conservation was planned.

- **Cereals pushed onto slopes (legacy).** In the colonial period, settlers took the fertile plains and local farmers moved onto unprotected hillslopes. After independence the process continued through population pressure, unsuited mechanisation and the spread of cereals even into arid zones (SRC-TG-803). In the Dorsale, rainfed cereals on slopes caused strong erosion of marl soils (SRC-TG-803).
- **Tillage and monoculture.** In the north-west, deep ploughing in spring and summer, stubble ploughing and repeated tillage before sowing go with continuous wheat and barley. Field crops have the lowest topsoil organic carbon of any land use (SRC-TG-808). Low organic matter weakens soil structure and raises runoff, which feeds erosion. SOC loss is therefore treated here as part of the same problem, not a separate one.
- **The decadal CES strategies (legacy decision).** Tunisia ran successive national CES strategies, the second ending in 2011. The 2020 PAN-LCD judges them harshly (SRC-TG-800):
  - targets were overestimated and out of proportion with national capacity;
  - objectives mixed social employment, water harvesting and soil protection;
  - many works were built without a scientific basis;
  - mechanical bench terraces failed in parts of the south;
  - underfunding and underestimated unit costs limited what was achieved.

  An earlier failure is also on record: the State started CES works before research results were in, and marl lands treated with physical works eroded faster in the 1969 flood (SRC-TG-803).
- **Tenure and maintenance.** Conservation works need continuity across neighbouring plots. Small, fragmented farms and land tenure problems block this (SRC-TG-800, SRC-TG-804). Farmers rarely maintain works on their land (SRC-TG-804). By 2011 the farmer groups set up under the CES law covered only 61% of the hill reservoirs meant for use (SRC-TG-800). The CES Code itself is not applied (SRC-TG-800).

## Evidence

- **Extent.** 3.56 Mha are affected by water erosion, 71% strongly to very strongly eroded (MEDD/CNEA 2007), and 50% are threatened by acute erosion with fertility loss (DG-ACTA 2010). The priority area is 2.718 Mha: 17.5% of the territory and 28% of agricultural land (DGATA 2017). All of these are cited in SRC-TG-800.
- **Loss rate.** The equivalent of 11,000 ha of productive land is lost per year to water erosion (SNDD 2014, cited in SRC-TG-800).
- **Measured erosion.** The specific erosion of 24 small catchments, derived from reservoir siltation, was 1.4-24 t/ha/yr in 1993-1999. In 1994-2004, one reservoir in six silted completely, one in four lost over 60% of capacity and one in three over 40% (SRC-TG-802). Cultivation drives erosion significantly once it covers more than 50% of a catchment on marl or clay soils with moderate to steep slopes (SRC-TG-802).
- **Downstream.** In 2021 large dams had lost on average 22.59% of their capacity to siltation (SRC-TG-881).
- **Organic carbon.** In the Wadi Beja watershed, topsoil SOC averaged 0.70% under field crops, 0.74% under grazing land, 0.87% under permanent crops and 1.09% under forest (SRC-TG-808). Loss of organic matter reduces permeability and raises runoff and erosion risk (SRC-TG-808). The same paper cites Brahim et al. (2010) for the claim that Tunisia has one of the highest SOC depletion rates in the Mediterranean (second-hand). Two north-western soils were resampled over 48 years (1971-2019): a Cambisol under cereals and a Luvisol under forest. Both lost SOC and nitrogen, mostly in the surface layer, and the cereal Cambisol lost more in all layers (SRC-TG-809, abstract only, no magnitudes; soil type and land use are confounded).
- **Conservation effort.** About 25% of the utilised agricultural area has been treated (SRC-TG-800). In 1999-2001 alone, 230,000 ha of watersheds were treated and 65,000 ha of existing works and plantations were maintained (SRC-TG-804).
- **Cost.** The World Bank puts the economic impact of land degradation at 2-7% of GDP per year (SRC-TG-881). An ICARDA analysis puts the cost of inaction on agricultural resource degradation at about USD 2.17 billion, 4.65% of GDP per year, with cropland accounting for 53.98% of the loss (SRC-TG-812). The two figures differ in scope (all land degradation vs cropland, irrigation water, rangeland and forest) and in method. Neither breaks out water erosion alone.
- **Projection.** Under SSP3, climate change could degrade Tunisia's critical ecosystems and soils by a further 28% by 2050, linked to more soil erosion; the illustrative map covers the Siliana watershed (SRC-TG-881). The climate projections disagree. The PAN-LCD cites +2.1 °C and 10-30% less rainfall by 2050 (SRC-TG-800). The CCDR worst case is +2 to 2.3 °C and 1-14% less rainfall by 2050, and the INM gives -6% (RCP 4.5) to -9% (RCP 8.5) (SRC-TG-881). See FND-TG-880 for the climate projections themselves. Both projections are modelled and secondary, so they are kept as context and this is not filed as a separate predicted finding.

## Why confidence is what it is

Medium. The national extent figures all come through one 2020 government document. That document cites 2007-2017 studies that were not opened, and it says itself that the quantification is "peu précis et surtout non actualisé" (SRC-TG-800). The measured erosion rates are solid but local: 24 small catchments, a conference paper (SRC-TG-802). The SOC evidence is cross-sectional in one watershed, plus an abstract with no numbers. The diagnosis of why the CES strategies fell short is the government's own and is consistent across the 2002 (SRC-TG-804), 2006 (SRC-TG-803) and 2020 (SRC-TG-800) documents. The direction of the problem is well supported; its exact size is not. Trend is set to unknown: no national time series of eroded area or soil loss was found. The 48-year SOC decline (SRC-TG-809) and the CCDR projection (SRC-TG-881) point toward worsening, but neither measures national erosion over time.

## Links to other sectors

- **Water.** Eroded soil ends up in reservoirs. Large dams had lost 22.59% of their capacity by 2021 (SRC-TG-881), and hill reservoirs in the Dorsale can silt up within a decade (SRC-TG-802). This shrinks surface water storage for irrigation and drinking water. The siltation itself belongs to the water lane (theme surface-water); this finding covers the on-farm cause.
